# From user needs to tested design decisions

Subject: Design · Unit: Design cycle · Revision: 2026-10-21.3

**Review draft — not independently approved. Practice marks are editorial, not official exam marks.**

Original MYP-oriented design-process practice using a fictional library wayfinding problem. Public framework links only; no completed portfolio, official criterion mark, manufactured prototype or user-study result is claimed.

## Prerequisites

- Distinguish an observation from a preference or assumption.

- Read a simple comparison table and explain a reasoned choice.

## Objectives

- Translate user evidence into measurable design requirements.

- Compare alternative ideas against justified criteria and constraints.

- Plan ethical tests and use results to evaluate and revise a solution.

## Investigate the problem before choosing a product

A design problem describes a gap between what people need to do and what the current situation allows. In a fictional school library, new students may struggle to find the returns desk. The problem is not automatically 'we need an app'. That wording chooses a solution before investigating the need. Ask who experiences the difficulty, in what setting and with what consequences. Observing routes, examining existing signs and asking users about confusing points can reveal different causes that require different solutions.

Separate evidence from assumptions. A student saying 'the sign is hard to read' is a report to investigate, not a complete measurement of legibility. Observe where the sign is viewed, what lighting and language demands exist, and whether the destination is named consistently. Research may include primary evidence collected with appropriate consent and secondary evidence from credited sources. Do not invent interviews or present fictional teaching data as observations. In a school context, follow the relevant adult guidance on participation and privacy. Collect only information needed for the design question, and avoid storing names or sensitive details when anonymous feedback is sufficient.

## Turn needs into a testable specification

A design specification turns findings into requirements that can guide both creation and evaluation. 'The sign should be good' cannot be tested. A more useful requirement might specify that a first-time visitor should identify the returns direction from an agreed viewing point, under the library's usual lighting, without extra prompting. The exact success threshold should be justified before testing rather than selected afterwards to make a design pass. A measurable requirement states the quantity or observable behaviour, the test conditions and the criterion for success.

Distinguish a requirement from a constraint and a preference. A requirement describes what the solution must achieve; a constraint limits the permitted solution space, such as a fixed noticeboard size or a school rule about wall attachment. A preference may influence desirability without being essential. These categories can overlap, but documenting their roles helps handle trade-offs. Requirements may address usability, accessibility, materials, maintenance, cost and environmental impacts. Do not claim compliance with a technical accessibility standard from a few classroom observations. A practice test can identify barriers without certifying that every user or formal standard has been addressed.

## Develop alternatives and explain the choice

Develop genuinely different ideas rather than minor colour variations of a single favourite. For the library problem, alternatives might include a clearer wall sign, a floor-level route marker where permitted, or a simple map at the entrance. Annotated sketches can show placement, text, dimensions and the intended sequence of use. Explain how each idea responds to the evidence. A sophisticated digital solution may introduce maintenance, device access or privacy problems that a static sign avoids; a static sign may be difficult to update when the layout changes.

A decision matrix can make comparisons explicit by scoring alternatives against criteria with justified weights. Multiply each score by its weight, then sum. The total is a decision aid, not an objective truth: scores and weights reflect judgements and can hide uncertainty. Test whether a small change in a weight reverses the ranking. Also screen mandatory constraints before comparing preferences. An idea that cannot legally or safely be installed does not become feasible merely because it scores well for appearance. Keep the reasoning and rejected alternatives visible so a reader can understand the choice rather than only seeing a final total.

## Plan and create an informative prototype

A prototype is a deliberately limited representation used to answer a question about a proposed design. A paper mock-up may test wording and information hierarchy before any durable sign is produced. A clickable screen may test navigation without having a working database. State what the prototype represents and what it cannot establish. If a paper model uses a larger type size than the planned final sign, a successful readability test on that model may not transfer. Prototype fidelity should match the question being investigated, not simply look impressive.

Plan the sequence of work, materials or tools, dimensions, time and checks required. Record changes made during creation and explain why they were necessary. A construction log should connect decisions to requirements, not merely list activities. Use teacher-approved tools and procedures for any physical work; this lesson does not supply a fabrication safety procedure. For a digital mock-up, use fictional data and avoid collecting real credentials. A working appearance is not evidence that a product handles errors, protects information or serves different access needs. Test the particular functions that have actually been implemented.

## Test fairly against the original requirements

A useful test connects a requirement to an observable task. For the library example, a participant might be asked to identify the direction of the returns desk from the agreed location without hints. Record success, time if relevant, where confusion occurred and any comments. Specify the success definition before testing: pointing correctly is different from walking to the destination. Avoid leading questions such as 'Was our clear new sign helpful?' because they encourage agreement. A neutral prompt asks what the person understood or where they expected to go.

Use participants and conditions appropriate to the claim, with required consent and privacy safeguards. Repeated trials with the same people may improve because they remember the route, not because the design improved. Consider different but comparable participants or counterbalanced orders when the school setting permits. Small convenience samples provide useful feedback but do not establish universal usability. Keep failures and assistance visible rather than excluding them to raise a success rate. Qualitative comments can explain why a numerical result occurred; a percentage alone may conceal an important barrier experienced by a particular user.

## Evaluate, iterate and communicate limitations

Evaluation compares the evidence with each requirement, explains differences and identifies changes worth making. If 7 of 10 fictional participants identify a direction correctly, the observed success rate is 70%. Whether that meets the specification depends on the pre-set threshold and conditions. Even a passing result does not prove accessibility for everyone or reliability in every setting. State which requirements were tested, which remain untested and what the prototype's limited fidelity prevents you from concluding.

An improvement should name the failure it addresses and a follow-up test. If users confuse 'returns' with 'collections', changing the wording or adding a tested symbol may address that confusion; adding decorative animation does not directly address it. Changing multiple features at once may improve the product but make it harder to identify which change helped. Document the trade-off. The design process is iterative: evaluation can lead back to research or specification, not only to polishing the final object. A clear report distinguishes proposed, created, tested and demonstrated states, credits external resources and keeps the evidence behind each claim available.

## Turning mixed prototype evidence into a decision

A prototype can meet one requirement and fail another. Evaluate each requirement against a stated threshold instead of combining unlike measurements into an unexplained overall score. If a safety-critical or essential requirement fails, high performance elsewhere does not automatically compensate. A small fictional user sample can reveal a problem but cannot establish that a design works for every user. Record who participated, what task was attempted and what assistance was given. Choose an iteration that addresses an observed failure, then repeat the relevant test under comparable conditions. A clearer label or larger control is a proposed repair until the revised prototype is actually tested.

## Turn conflicting requirements into explicit decisions

Design tests should connect to measurable requirements and the context in which users will work. A strong overall preference score does not compensate for failing a mandatory constraint. When two desirable properties conflict, state the trade-off and propose a test that can discriminate between alternatives. Repeated trials with the same user are useful for consistency but do not automatically expand the range of users represented. Report failures alongside successful trials and separate what a prototype demonstrates from what a later production version is expected to do.

## Worked example 1

Rewrite 'Make a library app' as a problem statement.

1. Identify the user task: first-time visitors need to find the returns desk.

2. Identify the obstacle without choosing a technology.

3. Leave multiple solution types available for investigation.

Answer: First-time visitors need to identify a route to the returns desk without individual staff directions.

## Worked example 2

Improve 'The sign must be easy to use' into a testable classroom requirement.

1. Choose an observable task and location.

2. Define a success threshold before testing and justify it for the project.

3. Specify no extra hints and record unsuccessful attempts as well.

Answer: Example requirement: at least 8 of 10 consenting test participants identify the returns direction without hints from the agreed entrance point under usual lighting. This is a project criterion, not a universal usability standard.

## Worked example 3

Compare fictional ideas A and B. Weights are clarity 3 and maintenance 2. A scores 4 and 2; B scores 3 and 4.

1. A total = 3 × 4 + 2 × 2 = 16.

2. B total = 3 × 3 + 2 × 4 = 17.

3. B leads under these judgements; check mandatory constraints and whether modest weight changes affect the ranking.

Answer: B scores 17 versus A's 16 under the stated weighting.

## Worked example 4

A paper mock-up uses large print, but the planned sign is half the size. What does a successful test establish?

1. It supports only the tested representation and conditions.

2. The smaller final text may be harder to read.

3. Test a correctly scaled version from the intended viewing point before transferring the claim.

Answer: The test does not establish readability of the smaller final sign.

## Worked example 5

Invented test results show 7 successes in 10 attempts against a pre-set requirement of 8 successes. Evaluate.

1. Observed success is 7/10 = 70%.

2. The requirement is not met; do not lower the threshold after seeing results.

3. Inspect failure observations, revise a relevant feature and plan another comparable test.

Answer: The fictional prototype fails this stated requirement and needs targeted revision and retesting.

## Worked example 6

A fictional prototype must support 2 kg and let at least 4 of 5 test participants open it unaided within 10 seconds. It supports 2 kg, but only 3 participants meet the opening criterion. Evaluate the two requirements and propose a targeted retest.

1. Compare the measured 2 kg support with the load threshold.

2. Compare 3 successful participants with the required minimum of 4.

3. Retest the revised opening design under the same assistance and timing rules; do not lower the threshold after failure.

Answer: The load requirement passes; the opening requirement fails. Revise the opening mechanism or instructions and repeat the unaided timed task with the threshold retained.

## Misconceptions

- A polished final product proves the design process was successful.
  Correction: Appearance alone does not show that user needs, constraints and test requirements were met.

- The highest decision-matrix total is objectively best.
  Correction: Totals depend on subjective scores and weights and cannot override mandatory constraints.

- A small successful test proves universal accessibility.
  Correction: It supports only the tested tasks, participants and conditions; broader claims require broader evidence.

## Flashcards

### Why avoid choosing a product in the problem statement?
It can hide the actual need and prematurely exclude better alternatives.

### What makes a design requirement testable?
An observable quantity or behaviour, stated conditions and a pre-set success criterion.

### Requirement versus constraint?
A requirement states what must be achieved; a constraint limits permitted solutions.

### Why document rejected ideas?
They show the alternatives and evidence behind the chosen approach.

### How is a weighted decision score calculated?
Multiply each criterion score by its weight and sum the products.

### What does sensitivity checking reveal?
Whether reasonable changes in weights or judgements alter the ranking.

### What is a prototype for?
Answering a specific design question using a limited representation.

### Why state prototype limitations?
Success on the model may not transfer to unimplemented functions or different scale and conditions.

### What makes a test question leading?
Its wording suggests the desired response or assumes the design already works.

### Why can repeated participants bias a route test?
They may remember the destination independently of the new design.

### What should an improvement be linked to?
An observed failure or limitation and a planned test of the change.

### Why preserve unsuccessful attempts?
Removing them would misrepresent the evidence and conceal barriers.

### Why retain separate requirement results?
A combined score can conceal an essential failure and mix incompatible measurements.

### When does a proposed design repair become evidence of improvement?
After the revised design is tested against the relevant requirement under a defensible comparison.

## Practice 7 — extension · 4 marks

The same five students test an old route map and then a new one. They are faster on the second attempt. Give an alternative explanation and improve the comparison.

Answer: They may remember the route. Use an appropriately planned comparison with comparable new participants or counterbalanced order, and retain task and condition details.

1. The second trial differs in prior experience as well as map design.

2. Learning the route can reduce time without any map improvement.

3. A revised design should address order or experience while respecting consent and practical constraints.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify learning or order effects.

- Connect the effect to the outcome.

- Propose a relevant comparison improvement.

- Avoid claiming the revision eliminates all uncertainty.

## Revision checklist

- Write a solution-neutral problem statement from evidence.

- Make requirements observable and distinguish constraints.

- Explain a design choice and its sensitivity to judgement.

- Link each evaluation claim and proposed improvement to test evidence.

## Revision guide

Design connects user evidence, explicit requirements, justified alternatives, an appropriate prototype and honest evaluation. The quality of the reasoning matters alongside the artefact; a convincing appearance is not a substitute for testing.

### Key ideas

- Investigate the need before deciding what to build.

- A decision matrix makes judgement visible but does not eliminate it.

- Prototype fidelity and participant selection limit what a test can establish.

- An iteration should respond to a named gap and include a plan to test the change.

### Weighted score = Σ(weight × criterion score)
Combines explicitly chosen judgements for comparison.
Conditions: Use a consistent scoring direction and screen mandatory constraints first.

### Observed success rate = successes / attempts × 100%
Describes the recorded attempts in the test.
Conditions: Do not drop failures or generalise to all users without supporting evidence.

### Retrieve without looking

- Convert one vague requirement into a measurable one.

- Explain how a high-scoring idea might still be infeasible.

- Describe a prototype limitation and an alternative explanation for apparent improvement.
