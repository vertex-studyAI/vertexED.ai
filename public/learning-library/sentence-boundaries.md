## Objectives

## Clauses before punctuation

An independent clause can function as a complete sentence because it expresses a complete grammatical thought. A dependent clause contains a subject and verb but is subordinated, often by a word such as because, although or when. 'Although the rain stopped' leaves the expected main statement unfinished. Punctuation cannot always repair a fragment without changing the structure: attaching an appropriate independent clause may be necessary. Before selecting a comma, semicolon or period, identify the boundaries of the clauses. Long clauses are not automatically incomplete, and short clauses can be complete; length is not the deciding feature.

## Joining complete thoughts

Two independent clauses can be separated by a period or joined by a semicolon when their relationship is clear. A comma alone between them normally creates a comma splice in standard edited English. A comma with a coordinating conjunction such as and, but or so can join them. An adverb such as however is not a coordinating conjunction: 'The route was longer; however, it was safer' uses a semicolon before the transition. A colon can introduce an explanation or example after a complete introductory clause. Do not insert a colon merely because a list follows if it separates a verb from its required complement.

## Meaning controls the transition

Transitions signal relationships such as contrast, consequence, addition or illustration. Choose the relationship by reading both sides, not by selecting a sophisticated-sounding word. However signals contrast; therefore signals a conclusion or consequence; for example introduces an instance of a broader statement. The punctuation around a transition depends on its grammatical position, so choosing the right meaning does not finish the editing task. Nonessential information can be enclosed with paired commas or dashes, but removing it should leave the basic sentence intact. During revision, test each proposed edit by reading the entire sentence and checking that punctuation does not separate a subject from its verb or create an unsupported logical connection.

## A systematic editing routine

When a sentence feels awkward, separate grammar from style. First identify the main subject and finite verb in each clause. Next mark any subordinating word, coordinating conjunction or introductory phrase. Then decide which boundaries need punctuation. This sequence prevents an attractive punctuation mark from concealing a structural problem. A semicolon cannot turn a dependent clause into an independent one, and adding more commas cannot supply a missing main clause.

Read the sentence after removing a parenthetical phrase. If the remaining core is grammatical and preserves the essential identification, paired commas or dashes may be appropriate. If removing the phrase changes which person or object is meant, the information may be restrictive and should not automatically be set off as optional. Context matters: the same words can be essential in one situation and additional in another. In exam-style editing, prefer the option that meets the stated meaning and grammatical requirements without introducing ambiguity. Concision means removing unnecessary wording, not deleting a connection needed for the logic.

## Repairing a comma splice while preserving the relationship

A sentence boundary repair must preserve meaning as well as grammar. Identify each independent clause by its subject and finite predicate before deciding how to join it. Two independent clauses can become separate sentences, can be joined with a semicolon, or can use a comma plus an appropriate coordinating conjunction. Choosing because or although changes the relationship by subordinating one clause, so use that repair only when the intended logic supports it. A transition such as however is not a coordinating conjunction. Read the repaired sentence aloud for sense, then check the clause structure rather than relying only on where a pause sounds natural.

## Check the clause before choosing a mark

Punctuation reflects grammatical relationships. A semicolon normally links independent clauses; a colon normally follows a complete clause and introduces an explanation, list or elaboration. A dependent clause beginning with although or because cannot stand as a complete sentence in standard edited prose unless it is intentionally used as a fragment in a context that permits one. Do not rely only on how long a pause sounds. Identify each clause's subject, finite verb and dependency, then choose punctuation that preserves the intended relationship.

## Worked example 1

Repair: 'The sample was small, the estimate was uncertain.'

1. Both parts are independent clauses.

2. A comma alone is insufficient in standard edited prose.

3. Use a semicolon, period or a suitable coordinating conjunction.

Answer: The sample was small, so the estimate was uncertain. Other grammatically sound repairs may also be valid.

## Worked example 2

Complete the relationship: 'The first method was quicker. ___, the second produced fewer errors.'

1. The second statement contrasts speed with accuracy.

2. Choose a contrast transition rather than a consequence transition.

3. Set off the introductory transition appropriately.

Answer: However, the second produced fewer errors.

## Worked example 3

Compare 'The students who revised passed' with 'The students, who revised, passed.'

1. In the first sentence the relative clause identifies the students being discussed.

2. In the second it is presented as additional information about the already identified students.

3. The punctuation can therefore change the claim, not merely the reading rhythm.

Answer: The first can distinguish revising students from others; the second presents the identified students as having revised and passed.

## Worked example 4

Repair the comma splice in “The first trial failed, the team repeated the measurement” in two ways that do not add a causal explanation.

1. The first trial failed is an independent clause.

2. The team repeated the measurement is also independent.

3. A full stop or semicolon supplies a valid boundary without asserting a new causal relationship.

Answer: The first trial failed. The team repeated the measurement. Alternatively: The first trial failed; the team repeated the measurement.

## Misconceptions

- A comma is correct anywhere a reader pauses.
  Correction: Punctuation follows grammatical relationships, not breathing alone.

- However joins clauses exactly like but.
  Correction: However is a transitional adverb and does not by itself license a comma between independent clauses.

## Flashcards

### What makes a clause independent?
It can function as a complete sentence expressing a complete grammatical thought.

### What is a comma splice?
Two independent clauses joined only by a comma in a context requiring a stronger boundary or conjunction.

### What can a semicolon connect?
Closely related independent clauses without a coordinating conjunction.

### What usually must precede a colon introducing an explanation?
A complete introductory clause.

### What should determine a transition word?
The logical relationship between the surrounding ideas, followed by a separate punctuation check.

### Why should you identify clauses before editing punctuation?
The grammatical relationship determines the boundary; punctuation cannot reliably repair an unidentified structural problem.

### Why is a pause not a sufficient punctuation rule?
Punctuation must fit the clause structure and relationship, which a spoken pause alone does not establish.

### What does because do to an otherwise independent clause?
It makes it subordinate, requiring a suitable main clause for a complete sentence in this context.
