## Parameters and repeated sampling

A population parameter is a numerical feature of the population or model of interest, such as a mean. A sample statistic is computed from the observed sample and is used to learn about that parameter. Different samples can produce different statistics even when they come from the same population. The sampling distribution describes this variation over repeated samples generated by a specified procedure. It is not the distribution of individual observations within one sample. Keep the target population, sampling procedure and statistic explicit before discussing an estimate's uncertainty.

## Standard deviation and standard error

A standard deviation describes spread in a distribution. The standard error of a statistic is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution. For the mean of n independent observations with common finite population variance σ², the standard error is σ/√n. When σ is unknown, replacing it with the sample standard deviation gives an estimated standard error under appropriate conditions. The square-root relationship means that halving this standard error requires four times the sample size, not twice. Dependence, clustering, unequal sampling weights or a different statistic can require a different calculation. State the assumptions rather than treating the formula as universal.

## Confidence procedures

A confidence interval is produced by a procedure designed to cover the fixed target parameter at a stated long-run rate under its assumptions. For a 95% procedure, approximately 95% of intervals constructed in repeated applications would contain the parameter if the procedure's conditions hold. After observing a particular interval, the fixed parameter is either inside it or outside it; the frequentist statement concerns the procedure's repeated-sampling performance. A Bayesian probability interval uses a different interpretation and requires additional modelling assumptions. Do not silently switch between these interpretations because both produce two endpoints.

## Uncertainty is not the same as bias

A narrow interval does not guarantee that the sample represents the intended population. A large convenience sample can estimate its own biased selection process very precisely while missing the target. Increasing sample size mainly reduces sampling variability under a valid design; it does not automatically repair nonresponse, measurement error or systematic exclusion. Report the estimate, uncertainty method, units and important assumptions together. Distinguish practical importance from statistical detectability, and avoid turning a failure to detect a difference into proof of exact equality. A useful analysis explains what additional data or design improvement would address the most important remaining limitation.

## Reducing sampling variation without repairing bias

Under an independent identically distributed sampling model, the standard error of a mean scales as the population standard deviation divided by the square root of sample size. Replacing the unknown population spread with a sample estimate adds an estimation step. Increasing sample size can reduce random sampling variation while leaving systematic selection or measurement bias intact. A large volunteer survey may give a precise estimate for its respondents without representing nonrespondents. State the sampling model before interpreting a narrow interval, and keep confidence in a procedure distinct from a probability that a fixed parameter moves between intervals. The arithmetic here isolates the standard-error scaling rather than certifying a real survey.

## Worked example 1

For independent observations with population standard deviation 12 and sample size 36, calculate the standard error of the mean.

1. Use σ/√n under the stated assumptions.

2. Compute √36=6.

3. Divide 12 by 6.

Answer: 2 in the same units as the observations.

## Worked example 2

Under the same independent-sampling model, what sample-size multiplier halves the standard error?

1. Standard error is proportional to 1/√n.

2. Doubling √n halves standard error.

3. Doubling √n requires multiplying n by four.

Answer: Four times the sample size.

## Worked example 3

A very large online volunteer survey gives a narrow interval for an attitude estimate. Explain one reason it may still poorly represent all residents.

1. Participation is self-selected rather than necessarily representative.

2. People with internet access or strong opinions may be included at different rates.

3. A narrow sampling interval does not by itself correct that selection bias.

Answer: Precision conditional on the sample does not establish representativeness of the target population.

## Worked example 4

Under an independent sampling model with population standard deviation 12 units, compare the standard error of the mean for n = 36 and n = 144.

1. For n = 36, standard error is 12/√36 = 2.

2. For n = 144, standard error is 12/√144 = 1.

3. Quadrupling sample size halves the standard error under this model.

Answer: The standard errors are 2 units and 1 unit respectively.

## Misconceptions

- The standard error is the spread of individual observations in one sample.
  Correction: It describes the sampling variation of a statistic; individual spread is a different quantity.

- A larger sample automatically removes selection bias.
  Correction: A biased sampling mechanism can remain biased at very large sample sizes.

## Practice 3 — standard · 2 marks

Explain why doubling sample size does not halve the standard error of the mean under the independent common-variance model.

Answer: Standard error scales with 1/√n, so doubling n multiplies it by 1/√2, not 1/2. Halving it requires four times n.

1. Identify the square-root dependence.

2. Compare the effect of doubling with the required fourfold increase.

## Practice 5 — extension · 3 marks

A researcher replaces a small representative sample with a huge convenience sample and claims accuracy must improve. Evaluate the claim.

Answer: The larger sample may reduce random variability within its selection process, but convenience selection can introduce systematic bias relative to the target population. Accuracy depends on the design and measurement as well as sample size, so the claim is not guaranteed.

1. Recognise the possible reduction in variability.

2. Identify the unresolved or increased selection bias.

3. Evaluate accuracy using both rather than sample size alone.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Discuss variability.

- Discuss bias.

- Give a qualified design-based judgement.
