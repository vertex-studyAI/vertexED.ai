## Constant ratio, not constant difference

A geometric sequence is generated by multiplying each term by a fixed number r. Starting with a = 5 and r = 3 gives 5, 15, 45, 135. Consecutive differences are 10, 30 and 90, so this is not arithmetic. Where the preceding term is nonzero, the ratio u_(n+1)/u_n reveals r. Always check more than one pair of terms; a single pair is consistent with many possible continuations. A stated geometric assumption is a mathematical rule, whereas an observed near-constant ratio is evidence for a possible model.

The sign and magnitude of r matter. With a positive first term and r greater than 1, terms increase. With 0 < r < 1, they decrease towards zero. A negative ratio makes nonzero terms alternate in sign; if its magnitude exceeds 1, their magnitudes increase. The ratio r = 1 produces a constant sequence. If a = 0, all generated terms are zero and division cannot recover r uniquely. The recurrence also permits r = 0, giving a, 0, 0, ..., but later ratios become undefined. Use the generating rule rather than dividing by zero.

## Building the nth term

Indexing from 1, the first term is a, the second is ar and the third is ar². Therefore u_n = ar^(n - 1) for a nonzero ratio. The exponent counts multiplication steps, not positions. Substituting n = 1 gives a because r^0 = 1. The recursive description u_1 = a and u_(n+1) = r u_n is often clearer for generating a table. For r = 0, state the first term separately and use the recurrence to avoid an ambiguous 0^0 at n = 1.

Recovering parameters requires attention to sign. If u_2 = 12 and u_4 = 48, division gives r² = 4, which allows r = 2 or r = -2 unless an additional condition rules one out. These lead to a = 6 or a = -6 respectively, and both reproduce the supplied even-position terms. An adjacent term or an explicit positive-ratio condition can resolve the ambiguity. If a target term requires solving an exponential equation, logarithms may help when their arguments are valid, but the resulting index still needs an integer and domain check. Do not silently discard a negative-ratio solution just because a calculator reports a principal square root.

## Deriving a finite geometric sum

Write S_n = a + ar + ar² + ... + ar^(n - 1). Multiplying by r shifts the powers: rS_n = ar + ar² + ... + ar^n. Subtract the second expression from the first; every interior term cancels, leaving (1 - r)S_n = a(1 - r^n). When r ≠ 1, division gives S_n = a(1 - r^n)/(1 - r). Multiplying the numerator and denominator by -1 gives the equivalent formula a(r^n - 1)/(r - 1). Keep both signs consistent rather than combining the numerator of one form with the denominator of the other.

The exponent in the sum formula is n, although the last individual term contains n - 1. The derivation explains this difference: the shifted expression ends at ar^n. If r = 1, the displayed fraction is undefined; add n copies of a to obtain S_n = na instead. Finite sums need no convergence assumption. Ratios greater than 1 or less than -1 are perfectly valid for a finite number of terms. This lesson does not use an infinite-sum formula, which has additional restrictions and must not replace a finite total.

## Bounds, repeated multiplication and interpretation

Sigma notation must match the indexing convention. The expression Σ from k = 0 to 4 of 3 × 2^k contains five terms: 3, 6, 12, 24 and 48. In this notation k = 0 is the first exponent, whereas u_1 is the first sequence term. The sum is 93. For a segment beginning at position p and ending at q, identify the new first term ar^(p - 1) and the number of terms q - p + 1. Apply the finite-sum formula to that shorter sequence, or subtract cumulative sums.

Separate repeated states from repeated contributions. If a container retains 80% of its contents after each idealised cycle, its remaining amount after t cycles is initial amount times 0.8^t. The initial amount is the state at cycle zero. Summing successive remaining amounts does not measure the amount removed; removal over those cycles is initial minus final amount. Conversely, if a pattern contributes a separate tile strip at each stage, summing stage contributions may be exactly what is required. A sound calculation begins by naming the quantity, the starting time or position, and whether values are being selected or accumulated.

## Testing a multiplicative model

A percentage change acts on the current value, not repeatedly on the initial value. A hypothetical quantity retaining 75% per cycle uses r = 0.75, not r = -0.25 and not subtraction of a fixed quarter of the initial amount. The sequence may produce fractional values even when real observations are counts. A model predicting 2.4 objects represents an approximation or expected quantity, not literally a fraction of an indivisible object. State which interpretation is intended and whether rounding at each stage changes the model.

Investigate the assumptions before extending the pattern. Growth may slow as space, material or demand becomes limiting; a retention rate may depend on changing conditions. Check ratios across the observed data, compare predicted values against measurements and describe deviations. A few nearly matching ratios do not prove a constant mechanism. Measurement error can strongly affect a ratio when the denominator is small, so context matters. A model can still be useful over a limited range even when it is not a universal rule. Prefer a qualified statement such as 'under the stated constant-ratio assumption' to a claim that every future value is known.

## Checks that expose common errors

Use independent checks that target structure. Generate the first few terms recursively and compare them with the explicit expression. For a short finite sum, add those terms directly. When all terms are positive, the total must exceed each individual term; if your computed sum is negative, inspect the signs in the formula. With negative ratios, a total may be smaller than individual magnitudes because cancellation occurs, so the positive-term check is inapplicable. Check r = 1 separately rather than trying to round an undefined result.

In a written explanation, show the first term, ratio, index convention and number of terms before substitution. Retain exact fractions where possible, especially for repeated powers, and round only the final requested quantity. For inverse problems, substitute every candidate ratio and first term into the given positions. If an alleged geometric list includes an internal zero followed by a nonzero term, no fixed finite multiplier can generate that transition. These checks help distinguish an incorrect rule from an arithmetic slip. During revision, mix decreasing, alternating and constant sequences instead of practising only positive doubling patterns.

## Negative ratios and alternating partial sums

A geometric sequence may change sign if its common ratio is negative. The sign pattern matters when adding terms: summing magnitudes answers a different question from summing signed terms. Before using a finite-sum formula, list a few terms to check the exponent and signs. The nth term contains n - 1 ratio multiplications, whereas the common finite-sum expression contains r raised to n. A ratio of -1 produces alternation without shrinking magnitudes; a ratio whose absolute value is below one produces shrinking magnitudes. Finite sums remain meaningful even when the sequence has no convergent infinite sum. Do not silently replace a finite task with an infinite-series formula.

## Derive a finite sum before taking any infinite limit

A finite geometric sum is defined even when the ratio is negative or has magnitude greater than one. The familiar derivation subtracts r times the sum from the sum, leaving only the first and final unmatched terms. Division by 1-r needs r≠1; the constant sequence must be handled separately. An infinite sum is an additional limit, available for a nonzero first term only when |r|<1. A finite total should never be replaced by the infinite value without discussing the missing tail and the required precision.

## Worked example 1

Find term 7 of 3, 6, 12, ...

1. a = 3 and r = 2.

2. u_7 = 3 × 2^6 = 192.

3. There are six multiplications after the first term.

Answer: 192

## Worked example 2

Find the sum of 5, 15, 45, 135.

1. There are n = 4 terms with a = 5 and r = 3.

2. S_4 = 5(3^4 - 1)/(3 - 1) = 200.

3. Direct addition gives 5 + 15 + 45 + 135 = 200.

Answer: 200

## Worked example 3

Given u_2 = 12 and u_4 = 48, find both possible real parameter pairs.

1. Divide to obtain r² = 4, so r = 2 or -2.

2. From ar = 12, the corresponding first terms are 6 and -6.

3. Both give u_4 = ar³ = 48; an extra sign condition would be needed to choose.

Answer: (a, r) = (6, 2) or (-6, -2)

## Worked example 4

An idealised process retains 80% of an initial 250 units each cycle. Find the amount after three cycles.

1. The initial state is cycle zero, so use exponent 3.

2. 250 × 0.8³ = 128.

3. The amount removed over the three cycles is 250 - 128 = 122, a different quantity.

Answer: 128 units remain

## Worked example 5

Evaluate Σ from k = 2 to 5 of 2^k.

1. The terms are 4, 8, 16 and 32, so there are four terms.

2. Use first term 4 and r = 2: S = 4(2^4 - 1)/(2 - 1).

3. The result is 60, agreeing with direct addition.

Answer: 60

## Worked example 6

A geometric sequence starts at 6 and has common ratio -1/2. List its first four terms and find their sum.

1. Multiply each term by -1/2 to get the next term.

2. Keep the alternating signs when adding: 6 - 3 + 1.5 - 0.75.

3. The signed total is 3.75.

Answer: Terms: 6, -3, 1.5, -0.75. Sum: 3.75.

## Misconceptions

- A 20% decrease means a ratio of -0.2.
  Correction: The retained fraction is 1 - 0.2 = 0.8. A negative ratio would alternate signs.

- The finite sum requires |r| < 1.
  Correction: That restriction belongs to a convergent infinite geometric series, not to a finite sum.

- r² = 9 implies r = 3 only.
  Correction: Both 3 and -3 satisfy it unless another condition excludes one.

## Flashcards

### What makes a sequence geometric?
A fixed multiplier generates each next term from the previous one.

### State the nth-term formula when r is nonzero.
u_n = ar^(n - 1), with first index 1.

### Why can recovering a ratio give two real solutions?
Even powers such as r² lose sign information; substitute both candidates.

### State the finite geometric sum for r ≠ 1.
S_n = a(1 - r^n)/(1 - r), equivalently a(r^n - 1)/(r - 1).

### What replaces the sum fraction when r = 1?
S_n = na because all n terms equal a.

### Why is r^n used in the sum formula?
Multiplying the sum by r creates a final shifted term ar^n before cancellation.

### What does a negative ratio do to nonzero terms?
Their signs alternate; the magnitude trend depends on |r|.

### Which multiplier represents retaining 65% each cycle?
0.65, applied to the current value at each cycle.

### How many powers run from k = 0 to k = m?
m + 1 powers, including the zero exponent.

### Does a finite geometric sum require convergence?
No. Any finite number of defined terms can be added.

### How should r = 0 be handled?
Use u_1 = a and the recurrence; later terms are zero, and avoid evaluating 0^0.

### Why can a sum of successive states be misleading?
The same quantity may be counted repeatedly; total removal is often initial minus final state instead.

### What does a negative common ratio do to nonzero geometric terms?
It alternates their signs.

### Why distinguish a signed sum from a sum of magnitudes?
Negative contributions reduce a signed total but add positively to a sum of magnitudes.

## Revision guide

Geometric change multiplies by a constant ratio. Count multiplication steps for an individual term and count included terms for a sum. Check exceptional cases, sign ambiguity and the meaning of the quantity before calculating.

### Key ideas

- A negative ratio alternates signs, not necessarily decreasing magnitudes.

- A finite sum is valid without an infinite-series convergence condition.

- State values and contributions cannot automatically be summed in the same way.

### u_n = ar^(n - 1)
The first term is multiplied n - 1 times.
Conditions: Index starts at 1; for r = 0 use the recurrence and state u_1 separately.

### S_n = a(1 - r^n)/(1 - r)
Finite total of n geometric terms.
Conditions: n is a positive integer and r ≠ 1.

### S_n = na
Sum of a constant sequence.
Conditions: r = 1.
