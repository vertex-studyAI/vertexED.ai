## Resources and enterprise

A business combines resources to supply goods or services. Land means natural resources, labour is human effort, capital is produced equipment used in production, and enterprise coordinates resources while taking decisions under uncertainty. Money can finance equipment but is not the machine itself. A bakery needs premises, workers, ovens and someone organising products, prices and operations. Entrepreneurs may seek independence, income, growth or a social purpose; a willingness to take risks is not a guarantee of success.

## Value and alternatives

For Cambridge’s added-value concept, subtract the cost of bought-in materials and components from sales value. A product sold for £18 using £7 of bought-in inputs adds £11 of value. That £11 is not automatically profit because wages, rent and other costs still need to be paid. Branding, convenience, quality or better design may increase what customers will pay, but extra expenditure can absorb the gain.

Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone. If a workshop can either make school desks or repair chairs with its available time, choosing desks sacrifices the best available chair-repair opportunity, not every imaginable project. Decisions need evidence about demand and capacity, not just enthusiasm for an idea.

## Objectives and a usable plan

An aim gives broad direction; a useful objective specifies a measurable outcome and time period. Survival may dominate for a new firm with uncertain demand, while an established firm may pursue market share or ethical sourcing alongside profit. A plan connects objectives with market research, operations, staffing, finance and cash needs. It should expose assumptions, such as expected weekly sales, and be revised when evidence changes. A detailed document cannot remove uncertainty or guarantee a lender’s support.

## Stakeholders can cooperate and conflict

Owners may want returns, employees secure pay and work, customers reliable value, suppliers prompt payment, and residents manageable noise and traffic. Expanding evening deliveries could increase sales but disturb neighbours and increase staff demands. A justified decision identifies which effects matter most in this business and proposes a feasible response, such as agreed delivery hours. Do not assume every stakeholder has equal influence or that all employees and all customers hold identical preferences. Success may require balancing long-term relationships with immediate financial survival.

## Worked example 1

A fictional café sells a sandwich for £5 using £1.80 of bought-in ingredients and packaging. Explain added value.

1. Subtract £1.80 from £5.

2. Recognise labour and overhead costs still have to be met.

Answer: £3.20 per sandwich before paying other operating costs; not necessarily profit.

## Worked example 2

Improve the objective: We want more customers.

1. Add a baseline, target and deadline.

2. Keep the measurement definition consistent so an apparent rise is not caused by changing the counting method.

Answer: Increase weekly repeat customers from 80 to 100 within six months, measured through the same loyalty system.

## Misconceptions

- Added value equals profit.
  Correction: Added value leaves other costs to be paid.

- A business plan guarantees success.
  Correction: It organises assumptions and decisions; demand, costs and competition can still change.

## Practice 1 — standard · 3 marks

[GCSE/IGCSE: untiered] Classify a bakery’s oven, baker’s labour and owner’s coordination using factors of production.

Answer: Capital, labour and enterprise respectively.

1. Classify by productive function, not ownership.

2. The oven is produced equipment; coordination is a distinct decision-making role.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Oven: capital.

- Baker: labour.

- Coordination: enterprise.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE/IGCSE: untiered] A workshop chooses desks rather than its next best use of time, chair repairs. Identify its opportunity cost.

Answer: The benefit forgone from chair repairs.

1. Identify the best rejected alternative.

2. Do not add all possible rejected projects together.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Chair repairs identified.

- Forgone benefit explained.

## Practice 3 — standard · 2 marks

[IGCSE: untiered] A product sells for £24 and uses £9 of bought-in inputs. Calculate added value and explain why profit cannot yet be known.

Answer: £15; labour and other costs have not been deducted.

1. 24 - 9 = 15.

2. Additional costs are needed for profit.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- £15.

- Missing costs explanation.

## Practice 4 — standard · 3 marks

[GCSE/IGCSE: untiered] Improve the objective to improve delivery performance by specifying a measure and deadline.

Answer: For example, raise the share of orders delivered within the promised window from 88% to 95% within three months.

1. Specify the performance definition.

2. Supply a measurable target and time period; accept other coherent examples.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Defined measure.

- Target.

- Deadline.

## Practice 5 — standard · 3 marks

[GCSE/IGCSE: untiered] A café proposes late-night deliveries. Explain one stakeholder benefit, one cost and a feasible compromise.

Answer: Owners may gain sales; residents may face noise; agreed delivery windows could reduce disturbance.

1. Identify different groups.

2. Connect the proposal to effects and a realistic response.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Benefit explained.

- Cost explained.

- Feasible compromise.

## Practice 6 — standard · 3 marks

[GCSE/IGCSE: untiered] Why should a start-up test sales assumptions even after producing a detailed business plan?

Answer: Forecast demand may be wrong; testing can reveal whether products, prices and capacity fit the market.

1. A plan contains assumptions rather than guaranteed outcomes.

2. Use feedback to adjust decisions and cash needs.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Uncertainty recognised.

- Testing value.

- Adjustment explained.
