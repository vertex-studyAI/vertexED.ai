# Causes, triggers, consequences and competing historical explanations

## Prerequisites

- Place events in chronological order.

- Distinguish a source statement from an inference.

## Objectives

- Separate a trigger from underlying conditions.

- Explain causal connections rather than list factors.

- Compare historical explanations using relevant evidence.

## Chronology is necessary but not sufficient

A cause must precede its effect in the relevant explanation, but an earlier event is not automatically a cause. Explain the mechanism connecting a condition or decision with the later outcome. A trigger may help explain why something happened at a particular moment, while longer-term conditions help explain why the situation was vulnerable to that trigger. These roles can interact. Avoid treating the final event in a timeline as the only cause simply because it is closest to the outcome, and distinguish an actor's intention from the consequence actually observed.

## Weigh factors using a stated criterion

An explanation can compare the scale, duration, reach or indispensability of different factors. State the criterion rather than repeatedly calling a factor important. Evidence that a condition existed does not by itself show how much difference it made. Consider the choices of different groups and the constraints within which they acted. A counterfactual question can clarify reasoning, but a claim about what would have happened without one factor remains an inference rather than a directly observed fact. Use it cautiously and support it with knowledge of the context.

## Interpretations are arguments with evidence

Two historians can agree about several events yet give different weight to their causes or consequences. Compare the question they address, the evidence they use and the reasoning that connects evidence to judgement. Do not reduce disagreement automatically to one author being biased and the other being objective. Sources and interpretations have purposes and limits that require specific evaluation. The examples here are fictional, so they teach explanation methods without inventing factual claims about an actual period. A board-specific course still requires its selected periods, depth studies and verified historical knowledge.

## Worked example 1

Fictional case: a town has years of water shortages, then a broken pump produces a one-day closure of its market. Distinguish an underlying condition and a trigger.

1. Long-running shortage is an underlying vulnerability.

2. The pump failure helps explain the immediate timing of the closure.

Answer: The shortage is a longer-term condition; pump failure is an immediate trigger.

## Worked example 2

Fictional records show a new bridge opened before trade grew. Explain why chronology alone is not enough to establish its effect.

1. A plausible mechanism is lower transport cost or easier access.

2. Other changes, such as demand or another transport route, could also explain growth.

Answer: Investigate mechanisms and alternative causes rather than equating earlier with causal.

## Misconceptions

- The last event before an outcome is its only cause.
  Correction: Long-term conditions and interacting decisions may also matter.

- A counterfactual is directly observable historical evidence.
  Correction: It is a reasoning tool whose plausibility needs justification.

## Flashcards

### What is a trigger?
An event helping explain the immediate timing of an outcome.

### What makes a causal explanation more than a list?
A stated mechanism linking factors to the outcome.

### Is an intended consequence necessarily an actual consequence?
No. Outcomes must be established separately.

### Why state a criterion of importance?
It makes the comparison of factors explicit and assessable.

### How can interpretations differ despite shared facts?
They can address different questions or assign different weights to evidence.

## Practice 1 — standard · 2 marks

In a fictional town, rising rents persist for years and a sudden eviction order prompts a protest. Identify a longer-term cause and an immediate trigger.

Answer: Rising rents are a longer-term condition; the eviction order is the immediate trigger.

1. Separate duration from immediate timing.

2. Explain how the order could mobilise existing grievances.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify both roles.

- Connect the trigger with the existing condition.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

A student writes: 'Poor roads caused low trade.' Add a plausible mechanism without claiming it is proven.

Answer: Poor roads could raise travel time, transport cost or damage risk, discouraging trade.

1. Identify an intermediate process linking roads and exchange.

2. Keep the explanation conditional until supported by evidence.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Give a causal mechanism.

- Avoid presenting a hypothesis as proven.

## Practice 3 — extension · 2 marks

One fictional interpretation stresses a policy's nationwide reach; another stresses its short duration. Explain why their judgements of importance may differ.

Answer: They use different criteria: scale of reach and duration of effect.

1. Identify each criterion.

2. Explain that a judgement should compare both criteria with relevant evidence rather than merely choose a label.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Name the two criteria.

- Explain how criteria influence judgement.

## Practice 4 — extension · 2 marks

A fictional reform was intended to reduce prices, but the only supplied record is the announcement. Can its success be established?

Answer: No. The announcement establishes an aim, not the achieved effect.

1. Distinguish intended policy from observed outcome.

2. Seek price evidence before and after, together with alternative explanations.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Separate intention from result.

- Identify evidence needed to assess effects.
