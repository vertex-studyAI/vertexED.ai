## From question to measurable variables

A geographical enquiry starts with a question about places, processes or spatial relationships. Turn broad terms into measurable variables before collecting observations. For example, accessibility might be represented by travel time to a service, but that measure does not capture affordability or whether the service meets users' needs. Define the spatial area, time period and population to which you want to generalise. State a hypothesis as a testable expectation, while recognising that a useful enquiry can investigate patterns without assuming the answer. A method should collect evidence capable of challenging the expectation, not merely confirming it.

## Sampling and comparability

Random sampling gives eligible units a known chance of selection when properly implemented. Systematic sampling selects observations at regular intervals, but can align accidentally with repeating spatial patterns. Stratified sampling divides the population into relevant groups and samples within them, helping ensure important groups are represented. Convenience sampling is easier but can exclude inaccessible or less visible areas. No method automatically eliminates all bias. Keep measurement procedures consistent between sites: instrument, duration, observer instructions and relevant conditions should be comparable. Repeated measurements help estimate variability but cannot fix a consistently miscalibrated instrument or an unrepresentative sampling frame.

## Patterns, explanations and limits

Maps and graphs can reveal clusters, gradients and outliers, but describe the evidence before explaining it. A relationship between distance from a centre and land use may reflect several interacting factors. Association does not alone identify the cause, especially when other variables also change across space. Use additional evidence and relevant theory to evaluate competing explanations. Distinguish random variation, systematic measurement error and sampling limitations because each calls for a different improvement. A conclusion should answer the enquiry within the sampled places and times, report exceptions and avoid extending a small local sample to every region without justification. Good evaluation proposes a specific feasible change and explains how it would improve the evidence.

## Data presentation and uncertainty

Choose a presentation that matches the enquiry and variable type. A map can reveal location and spatial pattern; a scatter plot compares two quantitative variables; a frequency display shows a distribution. A graph should identify units, scales and the data collection context. A truncated axis can exaggerate a small change visually, while an unsuitable scale can hide a useful pattern. Explain unusual values before deciding whether they are measurement mistakes or genuine features.

An average summarises a dataset but can conceal variation between sites or times. Report an appropriate measure of spread or show individual observations when that variation matters. Combining unequal-duration traffic counts without adjustment creates an unfair comparison: convert to comparable rates or collect equal-duration observations. Additional observations improve information only if they address the relevant uncertainty. Repeating one accessible site many times does not replace sampling different neighbourhoods. In a conclusion, distinguish what was observed, the explanation supported by the evidence and the limits on generalisation. An improvement should name a concrete change, the weakness it addresses and the expected benefit.

## Recognising clustered samples in a spatial comparison

A large number of measurements can still provide poor coverage if they come from one convenient location. Treat the sampling frame and the distribution of sites as part of the evidence. Repeated readings at one site may estimate local variability, but they do not automatically represent an entire river, town or coast. If the question compares land-use zones, distribute sampling across those zones and keep the measurement procedure consistent. Report access restrictions and missing sites so the reader can judge selection bias. A map of sampled locations often reveals clustering more clearly than a table of totals. ## Worked example 1

A town enquiry samples only shoppers leaving one supermarket at noon. Identify a generalisation problem.

1. The target population may include residents who use other shops or shop at other times.

2. The sampling frame excludes many of those people.

3. Sampling across locations and time periods would reduce this particular exclusion.

Answer: The convenience sample is unlikely to represent all town residents.

## Worked example 2

Traffic counts over equal five-minute intervals are 18, 22, 20 and 24. Find the mean count and state one limitation.

1. Sum the counts: 84.

2. Divide by four equal-duration observations to get 21.

3. These intervals may not represent other hours, days or seasons.

Answer: Mean 21 vehicles per five-minute interval; broader temporal generalisation is unverified.

## Worked example 3

Site A records 30 vehicles in five minutes; site B records 42 in seven minutes. Compare their average observed rates.

1. Divide each count by its observation duration.

2. Site A gives 30/5=6 vehicles per minute; site B gives 42/7=6.

3. The larger total at B does not imply a larger rate.

Answer: Both observed rates are 6 vehicles per minute.

## Misconceptions

- Repeating a measurement removes every kind of bias.
  Correction: Repetition helps describe variation but does not repair systematic error or an incomplete sampling frame.

- A mapped correlation proves a geographical cause.
  Correction: Alternative explanations and relevant processes must be investigated.

## Flashcards

### Why define a measurable version of a broad concept?
It makes the enquiry testable while clarifying what the chosen measure does and does not capture.

### What is the purpose of stratified sampling?
To sample within relevant population groups rather than accidentally omitting them.

### What is a potential weakness of systematic sampling?
The interval may coincide with a repeating spatial or temporal pattern.

### What can repeated measurements reveal?
Variability under the repeated conditions, not necessarily freedom from systematic error.

### What should limit the reach of a fieldwork conclusion?
The sampled population, places, times and measurement method.

### Why must observation durations be considered when comparing counts?
Unequal durations can produce different totals despite equal underlying rates; compare equivalent durations or rates.

### Why map sampling locations?
A map can expose clusters and missing areas that a large total sample count conceals.

### What can repeated readings at one site establish?
Variation or precision for that site under the measured conditions, not automatic representation of all sites.
