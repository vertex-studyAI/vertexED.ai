# Present-tense agreement and main-clause word order

Subject: German · Unit: Everyday communication · Revision: 2026-10-21.3

**Review draft — not independently approved. Practice marks are editorial, not official exam marks.**

Original beginner standard German examples. This lesson covers selected regular present forms and declarative main clauses, not a complete grammar or examination specification.

## Prerequisites

- Identify the subject and finite verb in a sentence.

- Recognise an infinitive ending in -en.

## Objectives

- Form selected regular present-tense verbs.

- Keep the finite verb in second constituent position in ordinary main statements.

- Distinguish familiar and formal address.

## Stem and subject agreement

For a straightforward regular present-tense verb such as wohnen, remove -en to obtain wohn- and choose an ending for the subject. The forms are ich wohne, du wohnst, er/sie/es wohnt, wir wohnen, ihr wohnt and sie wohnen. Formal Sie also takes wohnen. Written capitalisation helps distinguish formal Sie from other uses of sie, although context matters at the start of a sentence. Not every verb follows the simplest pattern without changes: stem changes, spelling adjustments and irregular verbs require their own treatment. Learn the subject and verb together rather than memorising a list of endings without their use.

## Second position means second constituent

In an ordinary German declarative main clause, the finite verb occupies the second constituent position. A constituent can contain several words, so second position does not always mean the second written word. Ich lerne heute Deutsch begins with the subject, while Heute lerne ich Deutsch begins with a time expression. In both cases lerne is the finite verb in second position. After placing another element first, do not leave the subject and finite verb in their original order automatically. This pattern is specific to the clause type being studied; questions and subordinate clauses need additional rules.

## Address and meaning

Du addresses one person familiarly; ihr addresses more than one person familiarly. Formal Sie can address one person or several people and uses the same verb form as third-person plural sie. The appropriate choice depends on relationship and context, not simply on counting people. The present tense can describe a routine or current situation, and time expressions help clarify the intended meaning. A direct English word-for-word substitution may miss both agreement and word order. Before writing, identify the intended audience, the subject of each action and the time or place information needed.

## Checking a connected description

Read a short paragraph clause by clause. Underline the finite verb, identify its subject and then check the first constituent. If the sentence starts with a time phrase, confirm that the finite verb still follows it directly in the main-clause pattern. Check nouns for initial capitals, as in Deutsch when it names the language. Do not introduce a complicated structure simply to make the paragraph look advanced; accurate connected information is more useful than an unexplained sequence of words. When correcting a sentence, state whether the problem concerns the verb ending, constituent order or vocabulary. These are separate decisions and one correction does not guarantee the others are right.

## Moving a time phrase without moving the finite verb out of second position

German main-clause word order counts constituents rather than individual words. A time phrase such as Am Montag occupies one position even though it contains two words. If it comes first, the finite verb remains in second position and the subject normally follows it. The verb ending still agrees with the subject; changing word order does not change wir into ihr. Keep the main-clause rule separate from subordinate-clause patterns, which are outside this exercise. For a regular present form, identify the stem and subject before checking position. This two-pass method prevents a grammatically correct ending from hiding a misplaced verb, or correct word order from hiding the wrong ending.

## Count sentence elements rather than individual words

In an ordinary German declarative main clause, the conjugated verb occupies the second position. The first position can contain a multiword phrase such as Am frühen Morgen, which counts as one sentence element. If that phrase comes first, the subject usually follows the finite verb. This rule differs from a yes/no question, which normally begins with the finite verb. Agreement still depends on the subject, even if it moves. These tasks use simple main clauses so that word order and regular present-tense endings can be checked separately.

## Worked example 1

Complete: Wir ___ in Berlin. Use wohnen.

1. Identify wir as first-person plural.

2. Use the present ending -en with wohn-.

3. Keep the finite verb after the first constituent wir.

Answer: Wir wohnen in Berlin.

## Worked example 2

Begin Ich lerne heute Deutsch with Heute without changing its basic information.

1. Move the time expression heute to first position.

2. Keep the finite verb lerne second.

3. Place the subject ich after the finite verb.

Answer: Heute lerne ich Deutsch.

## Worked example 3

Address one person formally and say You live in Hamburg.

1. Choose formal Sie rather than familiar du.

2. Use the formal present form wohnen.

3. Write the location after the verb.

Answer: Sie wohnen in Hamburg.

## Worked example 4

Rewrite “Wir lernen am Montag Deutsch” with “Am Montag” first, keeping the meaning and present tense.

1. Am Montag forms the first constituent.

2. The finite verb lernen occupies the second constituent position.

3. The subject wir follows; lernen still agrees with wir.

Answer: Am Montag lernen wir Deutsch.

## Misconceptions

- The finite verb must always be the second written word.
  Correction: The rule concerns constituent position; the first constituent may contain several words.

- Formal Sie uses the du ending when addressing one person.
  Correction: Formal Sie uses the plural-form conjugation, such as Sie wohnen, for one or several people.

## Flashcards

### Give the ich form of wohnen.
Ich wohne.

### Give the wir form of lernen.
Wir lernen.

### What occupies second constituent position in an ordinary declarative main clause?
The finite verb.

### Complete: Heute ___ ich Deutsch. (lernen)
Lerne: Heute lerne ich Deutsch.

### How does formal Sie conjugate wohnen?
Sie wohnen, whether addressing one person or several.

### What three separate checks help revise a beginner German main statement?
Identify the subject, choose the agreeing finite verb, and check its constituent position.

### Does verb-second mean the second written word?
No. It means the second constituent position in a declarative main clause.

### Does fronting a time phrase change the verb’s subject?
No. The finite verb still agrees with the same subject.
