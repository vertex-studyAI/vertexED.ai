# Claims, evidence, methods and justified uncertainty

## Prerequisites

- Distinguish a claim from a supporting reason.

- Recognise that methods answer different kinds of questions.

## Objectives

- Identify the kind of claim being evaluated.

- Compare the standards of evidence relevant to a question.

- Use a counterclaim to refine rather than automatically cancel an argument.

## Specify what is being claimed

A knowledge discussion becomes clearer when the claim and its scope are explicit. A statement about what happened, a judgement about what should happen and an interpretation of an artwork may call for different kinds of justification. Ask whether the claim is universal, probabilistic or limited to a context. A personal conviction is psychologically important but does not automatically provide publicly checkable evidence. Conversely, a claim is not false merely because someone holds it strongly. Separate the strength of commitment from the quality of the reasons offered.

## Match evidence and method to the question

Methods create opportunities and limitations. A controlled measurement can estimate an effect under specified conditions, while testimony may provide access to an event that cannot be reproduced. Mathematical proof establishes a conclusion from definitions and premises in a way that differs from a statistical estimate. These distinctions do not rank every discipline on a single simple ladder. Ask what a method can legitimately establish and what remains outside its reach. Reliability of a source, relevance to the question and sufficiency of the evidence are related but distinct considerations.

## Use uncertainty and counterclaims carefully

A counterclaim should engage the original reasoning, its assumptions or its scope. Finding one limitation does not automatically make all conclusions equally unsupported. A strong response may narrow the claim, distinguish cases or explain why a limitation matters more in one context than another. Acknowledge uncertainty proportionately: lack of certainty is not the same as lack of evidence. ## Worked example 1

Original claim: 'Because a forecast was wrong once, forecasts are useless.' Evaluate its scope.

1. One error challenges perfect accuracy, not necessarily all usefulness.

2. A forecast might still improve decisions compared with an explicit baseline, which needs evidence.

Answer: The universal conclusion is too strong; evaluate calibrated performance and intended use.

## Worked example 2

Two accounts disagree about a public event. One witness had a clear view but a personal stake; another had no stake but a blocked view. Compare the limits.

1. The first account raises a possible motivation issue, not automatic falsity.

2. The second has an access limitation despite apparent neutrality.

Answer: Evaluate access and possible incentives separately; seek corroborating evidence.

## Misconceptions

- A possible bias proves that every statement from a source is false.
  Correction: Bias is a reason to investigate influence and corroboration, not an automatic verdict.

- Any counterexample destroys every weaker version of an argument.
  Correction: It may refute a universal claim while leaving a narrower claim defensible.

## Flashcards

### Why state a claim's scope?
A universal claim needs different support from a bounded or probabilistic one.

### Is certainty identical to justification?
No. Confidence and quality of reasons can differ.

### What makes evidence relevant?
It bears on the specific question or claim being evaluated.

### What should a counterclaim address?
The claim's reasons, assumptions or scope.

### Does uncertainty mean all positions are equally supported?
No. Evidence can favour one position without guaranteeing certainty.

## Practice 1 — standard · 2 marks

Original claim: 'All eyewitness reports are inaccurate.' One independently corroborated accurate report is found. What follows logically?

Answer: The universal claim is false; this does not establish that every report is accurate.

1. Identify the universal quantifier.

2. Use the counterexample to reject that quantifier without reversing it into another universal claim.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Reject the universal claim.

- Avoid an unjustified opposite generalisation.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

A reliable thermometer measures a classroom's temperature. Does that reading alone establish whether a school rule is fair? Explain.

Answer: No. It may supply relevant factual context in some cases, but fairness requires additional criteria and reasoning.

1. Separate a physical measurement from a normative judgement.

2. Identify the missing evaluative principles and contextual facts.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Distinguish claim types.

- Identify additional justification needed.

## Practice 3 — extension · 2 marks

Compare checking one thousand examples of a mathematical conjecture with a valid proof covering all permitted cases.

Answer: Examples can support investigation and find counterexamples, but finite checking alone does not prove a universal conjecture over an infinite domain.

1. Distinguish empirical checking from deductive coverage.

2. Explain that a proof must address all cases within its premises.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- State the limitation of examples.

- Explain the role of a general proof.

## Practice 4 — extension · 2 marks

A model predicts well in one region but poorly in another. Give a proportionate revision of the claim that it works everywhere.

Answer: Its demonstrated usefulness is limited to the tested conditions where performance is adequate; the other region requires investigation.

1. Narrow the scope to supported evidence.

2. Ask which assumptions or data conditions differ rather than declaring universal success or universal uselessness.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Qualify the scope.

- Identify a useful next question.
