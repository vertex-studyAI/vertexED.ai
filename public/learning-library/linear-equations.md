## Equality and inverse operations

An equation asserts that two expressions have equal values. A solution is an input that makes that assertion true. Adding or subtracting the same quantity on both sides preserves equality, as does multiplying or dividing both sides by the same nonzero constant. Work toward isolating the variable, keeping every term attached to its sign. In 3(x-2)=12, divide by 3 before adding 2, or distribute first; both routes preserve the same solution set. A final substitution into the original equation checks arithmetic and any restrictions introduced along the way.

## Special cases and restrictions

For ax+b=c, the familiar formula x=(c-b)/a assumes a is not zero. When a=0, inspect the constant statement instead. A true statement such as 4=4 allows every input in the original domain, while a false statement such as 4=7 allows none. Cancelling an expression containing the variable can discard a solution or conceal an excluded input, so do not treat it like division by a known nonzero constant. An identity is an equation true for all inputs in its stated domain; an equation with a single solution is not an identity.

## Inequalities and a model's meaning

An inequality specifies a range, not usually one value. Addition and subtraction preserve its direction. Multiplication or division by a negative number reverses the direction because it reverses the order of numbers on a number line. Use an open endpoint for a strict inequality and a closed endpoint when equality is included. In a context, define the variable and units before writing the relationship. A continuous algebraic range may need restricting to whole numbers: if a budget allows n≤7.4 tickets, the maximum purchasable number is 7, not 7.4 or 8. Check that the final value answers the contextual question rather than merely reproducing an intermediate variable.

## Equivalence, exceptional cases and modelling checks

Solving an equation means finding every value in the stated domain that makes the original statement true. A reversible operation preserves exactly that solution set. Adding the same expression to both sides is reversible; multiplying both sides by zero is not. Dividing by an expression containing the unknown needs particular care, because that expression may equal zero at a genuine solution. Record any restriction before division and check whether the excluded case satisfies the original equation.

When the unknown cancels, stop interpreting the remaining statement as another instruction to isolate it. A true statement such as 6=6 means every permitted value satisfies that equation; a false statement such as 6=9 means none does. In a word problem, define both the variable and its units before translating the relationship. A charge per hour multiplied by hours gives a charge, so the equation should not add hours directly to currency. Finally, substitution checks the algebra, while a domain and units check tests whether the result answers the situation.

## Testing a budget boundary in the original model

A budget inequality combines a fixed charge with a variable charge and a domain restriction. Solve the algebra first, then impose the practical domain: a whole number of items cannot equal a fractional upper bound. Checking both the largest allowed integer and the next integer shows why rounding down is justified. The equality symbol also matters: at most includes spending the entire budget, whereas less than excludes equality. When dividing by a negative coefficient in a rearranged inequality, reverse the order relation. A final substitution in the original cost expression checks the sign handling and the interpretation together.

## Split parameter cases before dividing

An equation containing a parameter may describe several different solution patterns. In ax=b, divide by a only when a is nonzero. If a=0, the equation instead says 0=b, which is either always true or impossible. The same caution applies to a parameter multiplying an expression. Rational equations also carry original domain restrictions: clearing a denominator can produce a candidate that was never permitted. State these restrictions first and test every candidate in the original equation. A graph or substitution can check an answer but does not repair an invalid division.

## Worked example 1

Solve 5x-7=2x+11.

1. Subtract 2x from both sides: 3x-7=11.

2. Add 7 and divide by 3: 3x=18, so x=6.

3. Check: both original sides equal 23.

Answer: x=6.

## Worked example 2

A service costs 12 units plus 4 units per session. A learner has at most 39 units. How many whole sessions can they buy?

1. Let n be the nonnegative whole number of sessions; 12+4n≤39.

2. Subtract 12 and divide by 4 to obtain n≤6.75.

3. Six sessions cost 36; seven cost 40 and exceed the budget.

Answer: At most 6 sessions.

## Worked example 3

Solve 3(x+2)=3x+6 and compare it with 3(x+2)=3x+9.

1. Expand both left sides to 3x+6.

2. The first equation reduces to 6=6, true for every real x.

3. The second reduces to 6=9, which is false for every real x.

Answer: The first has all real numbers as solutions; the second has no solution.

## Worked example 4

A fictional club charges 17 units plus 6 per session. A learner can spend at most 70 units. Find the greatest whole number of sessions and verify the next number is unaffordable.

1. Let n be a nonnegative integer; 17 + 6n ≤ 70.

2. Then n ≤ 53/6, so the greatest permitted integer is 8.

3. Substitution gives 65 for 8 sessions and 71 for 9.

Answer: At most 8 sessions: 8 cost 65 units, while 9 cost 71.

## Misconceptions

- Moving a term across the equals sign is a new algebraic operation.
  Correction: It abbreviates applying the same inverse operation to both sides; show that operation when unsure.

- Dividing an inequality always preserves its direction.
  Correction: Division by a negative number reverses the inequality.

## Flashcards

### What does a solution of an equation do?
It makes the original equality true while respecting its domain.

### What happens if the coefficient of x becomes zero?
Inspect the remaining statement: true means all permitted inputs; false means no solution.

### When must an inequality sign reverse?
When both sides are multiplied or divided by a negative number.

### Why can cancellation lose information?
A cancelled factor might equal zero; its restrictions or possible zero solutions must be checked.

### How do you interpret n≤6.75 for a number of tickets?
For nonnegative whole tickets, n can be 0 through 6.

### What does an identity such as 6=6 mean after eliminating the unknown?
Every value in the original permitted domain satisfies the equation; it does not mean the unknown equals 6.

### How do you justify a whole-number budget maximum?
Check that the candidate satisfies the original budget and that the next whole number exceeds it.

### When does dividing reverse an inequality?
When dividing both sides by a negative number.
