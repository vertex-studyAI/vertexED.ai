## Represent the decision before calculating

A decision node marks a choice controlled by the business; a chance node represents uncertain outcomes. Probabilities from one chance node should sum to one when the outcomes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Label what a payoff measures and the time period it covers. A forecast of revenue cannot be compared directly with another option’s profit. If an initial cost is shown separately, specify whether endpoint payoffs are before or after that cost so it is not deducted twice.

## Expected value is a weighted mean

For a fictional launch, suppose high demand has probability 0.6 and returns £80000 before the launch cost, while low demand has probability 0.4 and returns £10000 before that cost. Expected return is 0.6 × 80000 + 0.4 × 10000 = £52000. A separate launch cost of £20000 gives expected net gain £32000. This is a probability-weighted average across hypothetical repetitions, not a promise that the one launch will earn exactly £32000. The low-demand net result is a £10000 loss.

## Work backwards and test assumptions

At chance nodes compute weighted returns; at decision nodes compare available choices using the chosen objective and any constraints. For a multistage tree, work from the endpoints back towards the first decision. Keep costs at the stage where they occur. Sensitivity analysis varies uncertain inputs to see when the preferred option changes. If a launch’s returns before cost are £80000 or £10000 and cost is £20000, expected net gain is 70000p - 10000, where p is the high-demand probability. This expression reveals how strongly the decision depends on the estimated probability.

## A justified choice needs more than the mean

A smaller business may reject a high expected gain if an adverse outcome threatens survival or if it cannot finance the initial cost. Estimates may be based on a small survey or on a market unlike the target market. Non-financial concerns such as reputation, employee wellbeing and environmental impact may be poorly captured by one monetary payoff. A decision tree organises assumptions and consequences; it does not remove the need for judgement. A strong evaluation identifies which uncertainty or constraint is decisive and what further evidence would change the decision.

## Worked example 1

Option A returns £60000 with probability 0.7 or £10000 with probability 0.3, before a £20000 cost. Find expected net gain.

1. Expected return = £42000 + £3000 = £45000.

2. Subtract the separate £20000 cost once.

Answer: £25000.

## Worked example 2

A launch has expected net gain 70000p - 10000. A safe option earns £18000. Find the probability at which they tie.

1. Set 70000p - 10000 = 18000.

2. 70000p = 28000, so p = 0.4; above that the launch has the larger expected net gain.

Answer: p = 0.4.

## Misconceptions

- Expected value is the guaranteed outcome.
  Correction: It is a weighted mean; a single decision produces one realised outcome.

- The highest expected gain is always feasible.
  Correction: Finance, downside risk and other objectives can constrain the choice.

## Practice 1 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] A project returns £90000 with probability 0.4 and £20000 with probability 0.6, before a £25000 setup cost. Find expected net gain.

Answer: £23000.

1. Expected return = 36000 + 12000 = 48000.

2. Subtract 25000 once.

## Practice 2 — extension · 3 marks

[A-Level: untiered] A chance node has exhaustive mutually exclusive outcomes with probabilities 0.5, 0.3 and 0.4. What must be corrected before using it?

Answer: The probabilities sum to 1.2, so the outcome definitions or estimates are inconsistent.

1. Sum the values.

2. Check overlap or incorrect estimates rather than silently normalising without justification.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- 1.2 identified.

- Consistency issue.

- Need to review assumptions.

## Practice 3 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Endpoint profits are already after a £10000 setup cost. Why is deducting £10000 again from their expected value wrong?

Answer: It counts the same cost twice.

1. Identify the meaning of endpoint profits.

2. Weighted after-cost outcomes already include the cost.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- After-cost interpretation.

- Double counting explained.

## Practice 4 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] A launch has expected net gain 50000p - 5000; an alternative gives £15000. Find the tie probability.

Answer: 0.4.

1. Set 50000p - 5000 = 15000.

2. 50000p = 20000, so p = 0.4.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Correct equality.

- 0.4.

## Practice 5 — extension · 3 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Why could a cash-constrained firm reject the option with higher expected net gain?

Answer: It may be unable to finance the initial cost or survive the downside outcome.

1. Separate average return from cash timing and risk.

2. Relate the constraint to this firm rather than claiming risk is always bad.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Finance or downside constraint.

- Mean versus realised outcome.

- Contextual reasoning.

## Practice 6 — extension · 3 marks

[A-Level: untiered] A launch probability comes from interviewing ten loyal existing customers, but the product targets new customers. Evaluate the estimate.

Answer: The sample is small and may not represent the target market; broader relevant research could change the probability.
