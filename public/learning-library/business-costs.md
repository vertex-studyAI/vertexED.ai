## Classifying costs within a stated range

A fixed cost does not change with output over the period and relevant range being considered. Rent might remain fixed during a lease even if production falls, but expanding into another building can increase it. A variable cost changes with output; in a simple linear model, each additional unit uses the same amount of material at the same price. Total variable cost equals variable cost per unit multiplied by output. Total cost adds fixed cost to total variable cost. Classification depends on context: labour may be salaried, paid per unit or include both elements. Always state the period and distinguish total amounts from amounts per unit.

## Contribution and break-even

Revenue equals selling price per unit multiplied by units sold. Contribution per unit is selling price minus variable cost per unit. Each sale contributes this amount toward fixed costs; once fixed costs are covered, additional contribution increases operating profit in the model. Break-even occurs where total revenue equals total cost. If contribution per unit is positive, break-even quantity equals fixed costs divided by contribution per unit. For indivisible products, round a non-integer result upward when finding the smallest whole number of units needed to avoid a loss. If contribution is zero or negative while fixed costs are positive, increasing output cannot produce break-even under these assumptions.

## Using a model without mistaking it for a forecast

The margin of safety is actual or forecast sales minus break-even sales, expressed in units or as a percentage of the stated sales figure. It describes how far sales could fall before the model predicts a loss. A larger margin does not guarantee survival: customers may pay late, costs may change and the business may lack cash despite recording profit. Break-even analysis usually assumes constant prices and unit variable costs, fixed costs within a relevant range, and that produced units are sold. Discounts, capacity constraints and a changing product mix can invalidate those assumptions. A sound recommendation combines the calculation with evidence about demand, capacity, quality and cash flow, rather than treating the break-even number as a sales forecast.

## Sensitivity and decisions under limited capacity

A break-even calculation is most useful when paired with sensitivity analysis: change one assumption and examine how the result changes. If fixed costs rise, the contribution needed to cover them rises. If selling price falls while variable cost stays unchanged, contribution per unit shrinks and more sales are required. These conclusions rely on contribution remaining positive and the business being able to make and sell the additional units.

Capacity can make a mathematically calculated break-even level operationally unreachable. A workshop capable of producing 80 units cannot cover a modelled 100-unit threshold without changing capacity, price, cost or product mix. A proposed expansion may itself increase fixed costs, so the threshold must be recalculated rather than assumed constant. For a decision, distinguish relevant future changes from costs already incurred, while following the question's stated model. Qualitative factors such as reliability, staff workload and customer response also matter. Finish a recommendation by identifying which assumption would most change the conclusion and what evidence would help test it.

## Capacity can rule out a calculated break-even point

A break-even calculation identifies a sales quantity under a stated cost and price model. It does not establish that a firm can produce or sell that quantity. Compare the result with capacity, expected demand and the relevant time period. If capacity is below break-even, calculate the loss at capacity before suggesting a change. Increasing price, lowering variable cost and reducing fixed cost affect different parts of the model; none is guaranteed to be feasible. Distinguish unit contribution from total profit: contribution first covers fixed costs. ## Worked example 1

A workshop has fixed costs of 600 currency units, a selling price of 15 and variable cost of 9 per item. Find break-even output.

1. Contribution per item is 15 − 9 = 6.

2. Break-even output is 600 ÷ 6 = 100 items.

3. At 100 sales, revenue is 1500 and total cost is 600 + 900 = 1500.

Answer: 100 items.

## Worked example 2

For the same workshop, calculate modelled profit and margin of safety at 140 sales.

1. Total contribution is 140 × 6 = 840.

2. Subtract fixed costs: 840 − 600 = 240.

3. Margin of safety is 140 − 100 = 40 items, or 40 ÷ 140 × 100 ≈ 28.6% of sales.

Answer: Profit 240; margin of safety 40 items, approximately 28.6%.

## Worked example 3

A product has fixed costs of 500 and contribution of 5 per unit, but maximum period capacity is 80 units. Evaluate break-even feasibility.

1. Break-even output is 500/5=100 units.

2. Capacity is below the required output.

3. At full capacity, contribution is 400 and modelled profit is -100.

Answer: Break-even is infeasible within current capacity under these assumptions.

## Worked example 4

A fictional workshop has monthly fixed costs of 1,200 units, price 18 per item and variable cost 10 per item. Capacity is 120 items per month. Find break-even output and profit at capacity.

1. Unit contribution is 18 - 10 = 8.

2. Break-even output is 1,200/8 = 150, above capacity 120.

3. Profit at capacity is 120 × 8 - 1,200 = -240.

Answer: Break-even output is 150 items; at capacity the workshop makes a loss of 240 units.

## Misconceptions

- Fixed costs can never change.
  Correction: They are fixed only within the stated time period and relevant range.

- Break-even output predicts how many customers will buy.
  Correction: It identifies a cost-revenue threshold, not demand.
