## What flows: both tiers

A conducting metal contains electrons able to move through its structure. A complete circuit with a source can sustain an electric current. The charge does not disappear inside a lamp: energy is transferred there, but the circuit still conserves charge. In a single unbranched loop the steady current is the same on either side of the lamp. Direct current has one direction; alternating current repeatedly reverses direction. A battery supplies direct current.

## Measurements: both tiers

An ammeter measures current in amperes and is inserted in series so the branch current passes through it. A voltmeter measures potential difference and is connected across the two terminals of the component. Start with a suitable range to avoid overload, then select a more sensitive range if safe. Connecting an ammeter straight across a supply can create a very low-resistance path and is unsafe.

Resistance at a particular operating point is R = V/I. A component with 6 V across it and current 0.5 A has resistance 12 ohms. Potential difference describes energy transferred per unit charge; it is not another name for current. This explanation introduces the meaning, while quantitative charge-energy work must follow the correct board tier.

## Current-voltage relationships: GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Extended only

For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, doubling potential difference doubles current, so the V/I ratio stays fixed. A filament lamp heats as current increases and its resistance rises, making its current-voltage relationship nonlinear. Explaining these current-voltage graph shapes is Cambridge Supplement material, not a Core requirement.

## Controlling a wire investigation: both tiers

Control temperature when testing wire length: heating changes resistance and could obscure the effect of length. Repeat readings to identify inconsistent measurements; do not average measurements from deliberately different conditions as if they were repeats. Keep the wire material and cross-sectional area unchanged while varying length.

## Charge calculation: GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Extended only

Current is charge passing a point per second, so Q = It. A steady 0.3 A for 40 s transfers 12 C. Convert minutes into seconds before substitution. Conventional current is defined in the direction positive charge would move; in an external metal wire the electrons move in the opposite direction. For Cambridge 0625 the quantitative current definition and this direction comparison are Supplement material. They should not be silently treated as Core recall requirements.

## Worked example 1

A resistor has 9 V across it and carries 0.3 A. Find resistance.

1. Use R = V/I.

2. 9 / 0.3 = 30; give the resistance unit.

Answer: 30 ohms.

## Worked example 2

A GCSE/IGCSE Extended circuit carries 0.25 A for 2 minutes. Find charge transferred.

1. Convert 2 minutes to 120 seconds.

2. Q = 0.25 × 120 = 30 C.

Answer: 30 C.

## Misconceptions

- A lamp uses up current.
  Correction: The lamp transfers energy; steady current is conserved around an unbranched loop.

- V = IR proves resistance is always constant.
  Correction: R = V/I can be evaluated at an operating point; R may change with temperature or other conditions.

## Practice 1 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Describe the two meter connections needed to measure a lamp’s current and potential difference safely in a low-voltage school circuit.

Answer: Ammeter in series; voltmeter in parallel across the lamp.

1. Trace the lamp branch for the ammeter.

2. Connect the voltmeter to the lamp’s two terminals.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] A wire has 3.6 V across it and a current of 0.12 A. Find its resistance.

Answer: 30 ohms.

1. R = 3.6 / 0.12.

2. Calculate 30 and attach ohms.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Correct substitution.

## Practice 3 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] The current before a lamp in a single loop is 0.4 A. Predict the current after it and explain.

Answer: 0.4 A; charge is not consumed by the lamp.

1. There is no branch at which current divides.

2. The lamp transfers energy rather than destroying charge.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- 0.4 A.

- Charge conservation explanation.

## Practice 4 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Why should a wire-length resistance investigation limit heating and keep material and diameter unchanged?

Answer: Each can change resistance independently of length.

1. Temperature affects resistance.

2. Material and cross-sectional area also affect it, so changing them confounds the comparison.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Temperature reason.

- Material and diameter controlled.

## Practice 5 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Extended only] A steady 0.6 A flows for 3 minutes. Calculate the charge passing a point.

Answer: 108 C.

1. 3 × 60 = 180 s.

2. Q = 0.6 × 180 = 108 C.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- 180 s.

- 108 C.

## Practice 6 — extension · 2 marks

[IGCSE Extended only] In a battery’s external metal circuit, compare electron-flow direction with conventional current.

Answer: Electrons move from negative towards positive; conventional current is opposite.

1. Electrons carry negative charge.

2. Conventional direction is based on positive-charge motion.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Electron direction correct.

- Conventional direction opposite.
