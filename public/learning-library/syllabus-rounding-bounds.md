## Both tiers: decimal places and significant figures

Decimal places count positions after the decimal point; significant figures start at the first nonzero digit. To round 0.006478 to two significant figures, keep 6 and 4 and inspect the next digit 7, giving 0.0065. To round the same value to three decimal places, keep through thousandths and inspect the next digit, giving 0.006. Leading zeros locate a value but do not count as significant figures.

Round only at the requested stage. Store the full calculator result through a multi-step calculation and round the final answer; premature rounding can change the last digit. State precision if trailing zeros could be ambiguous: 6.0 is explicitly two significant figures. In context a number of buses must be a whole number large enough for everyone, whereas a money answer is usually given to the nearest penny unless another rule is stated.

## Both tiers: estimation as an independent check

An estimate replaces awkward values by nearby convenient values, often one significant figure, and then calculates. For 29.8×4.12/0.203, using 30×4/0.2 gives 600. The estimate checks magnitude and decimal placement; it does not promise the final answer is 600 exactly. Round numerator and denominator carefully and recognise that their errors may reinforce or offset each other.

Check technological output against the problem: a time cannot be negative in a positive-distance journey, and a discount cannot increase a price. Keep enough working to distinguish an estimate from an exact computation. If a bound is needed, a casual estimate is not sufficient; use justified lower and upper limits instead.

## Both tiers: rounded intervals; GCSE: truncation

For a positive length reported as 8.2 cm to the nearest 0.1 cm, the rounding step is 0.1 and the half-step is 0.05. The interval is 8.15≤L<8.25 under the usual half-up convention for these examples. The lower endpoint is included and the upper is excluded because 8.25 rounds up to 8.3. The upper bound describes the boundary, not necessarily an attainable greatest measurement.

For positive truncation, discard digits rather than rounding: 8.2 cm truncated to one decimal place means 8.2≤L<8.3. Do not subtract a half-step for truncation. For 320 reported to the nearest ten, the rounding step is 10, giving 315≤x<325. Use the stated precision, not a guess from the written number. These questions use positive measurements; negative truncation and tie conventions require separately stated rules.

## Higher / Extended: calculated-result bounds

For positive independent measurements, the smallest sum uses both lower bounds and the largest sum uses both upper bounds. A difference a−b is smallest when a is small and b is large. Products of positive values increase with each factor, so area bounds use both lower or both upper endpoints. Division a/b with positive b increases with a but decreases with b: minimum speed uses least distance and greatest time, while maximum speed uses greatest distance and least time.

An upper boundary may not be attained because an input endpoint is excluded. Report a bound without claiming an actual maximum exists. Preserve exact endpoint values until the end. If a denominator can be zero or a factor changes sign, these simple positive-value rules are insufficient; inspect the domain first. To justify a rounded answer, check whether every possible result in the interval rounds to the same value. Cambridge Core stops at direct bounds of measured data, not this propagation.

## Worked example 1

Round 0.04786 to three significant figures and two decimal places. Give the interval for a mass reported as 2.7 kg to the nearest 0.1 kg.

1. Significant digits start at 4, so retain 4,7,8 then round up. Decimal places instead count from the point.

2. Half the measurement step is 0.05; subtract and add it around 2.7.

Answer: 0.0479; 0.05; 2.65≤m<2.75 kg.

## Worked example 2

[Higher / Extended] A rectangle has length 12 cm and width 5 cm, each rounded to the nearest cm. Find area bounds.

1. Use 11.5≤L<12.5 and 4.5≤W<5.5.

2. Both dimensions are positive, so multiply lower endpoints for 51.75 and upper boundaries for 68.75.

Answer: 51.75≤A<68.75 cm².

## Misconceptions

- The upper bound is always included.
  Correction: Rounding intervals generally exclude the upper endpoint under the stated convention.

- Largest speed uses largest distance and largest time.
  Correction: For positive values a larger denominator reduces the quotient; use smallest time for the upper speed bound.

## Flashcards

### Where do significant figures begin?
At the first nonzero digit.

### What is the half-step for nearest 0.01?
0.005.

### What interval gives positive x truncated to 4.6 at one decimal place?
4.6≤x<4.7.

### How is an upper positive quotient bound formed?
Upper numerator divided by lower positive denominator.

### When should intermediate calculator values be rounded?
Only if the problem requires it; otherwise retain them until the final result.

## Practice 1 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Round 0.003846 to two significant figures and four decimal places. Round 48,765 to the nearest thousand.

Answer: 0.0038; 0.0038; 49,000.

1. For significant figures keep 3 and 8; the next digit is 4.

2. For four decimal places keep through the 8; round 48,765 up to the next thousand.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Distinguish the two rules even though the answers coincide.

- Give 49,000.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Estimate 58.9×0.398/1.97 by rounding each value to one significant figure. Is a calculator result near 119 plausible?

Answer: Estimate 60×0.4/2=12; 119 is implausible by about a factor of ten.

1. Round the three input values separately.

2. Use the estimate to check the magnitude rather than exact last digits.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Obtain the estimate 12.

- Reject 119 using magnitude.

## Practice 3 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] A length is 4.37 m to the nearest 0.01 m. Give its lower and upper bounds and an inequality interval.

Answer: 4.365 m and 4.375 m; 4.365≤L<4.375.

1. The half-step is 0.005 m.

2. Subtract and add the half-step; exclude the upper endpoint.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Use the correct half-step.

- Give the endpoints with correct inequality signs.

## Practice 4 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] A count estimate is recorded as 12,400 to the nearest hundred. Give the rounding interval for its underlying value.

Answer: 12,350≤x<12,450.

1. The rounding step is 100, not 1 or 1000.

2. Use a half-step of 50 on both sides.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Select the stated precision.

- Give the correct interval.

## Practice 5 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher] A positive value is 7.38 truncated to two decimal places. Give its interval and compare with 7.38 rounded to two decimal places.

Answer: Truncated: 7.38≤x<7.39. Rounded: 7.375≤x<7.385.

1. Truncation removes positive tail digits without rounding.

2. Rounding centres an interval of width 0.01 on 7.38.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Give the truncation interval.

- Give the distinct rounding interval.

## Practice 6 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] A calculation gives 183÷16=11.4375 minibuses for 183 people, with 16 seats each. How many buses are needed? Explain why rounding to the nearest whole number is wrong here.

Answer: 12 buses; 11 have only 176 seats.

1. The number of buses must be an integer satisfying capacity.

2. Round upwards to cover the remaining 7 people.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Give 12.

- Use capacity rather than a generic rounding rule.

## Practice 7 — extension · 3 marks

[GCSE Higher; IGCSE Extended] A rectangle is 8.0 cm by 3.0 cm, each rounded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Find perimeter and area bounds.

Answer: 21.8≤P<22.2 cm; 23.4525≤A<24.5525 cm².

1. Use 7.95≤L<8.05 and 2.95≤W<3.05.

2. Compute P=2(L+W) at matching endpoints and A=LW at matching endpoints.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Give the two input intervals.

- Give correct perimeter bounds.

- Give correct area bounds.

## Practice 8 — extension · 3 marks

[GCSE Higher; IGCSE Extended] Distance is 150 m to the nearest metre and time is 12 s to the nearest second. Find lower and upper boundaries for average speed. Keep exact endpoint calculations or give five significant figures.

Answer: Lower 149.5/12.5=11.96 m/s; upper boundary 150.5/11.5≈13.087 m/s. Both extremal boundaries are unattained here.

1. Distance lies in [149.5,150.5) and time in [11.5,12.5).

2. Use smallest distance over largest time for the lower boundary, and largest distance over smallest time for the upper boundary.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Set up the correct quotients.

- Give both boundaries with units.

- Recognise excluded input endpoints prevent attainment.

## Practice 9 — extension · 2 marks

[GCSE Higher; IGCSE Extended] Two positive lengths are reported as a=10 cm and b=6 cm, each to the nearest cm. Find boundaries for a−b and explain which endpoints you combine.

Answer: 3<a−b<5 cm.

1. The least possible boundary is 9.5−6.5=3; the greatest is 10.5−5.5=5.

2. Each equality would require an excluded upper input endpoint, so neither is attained.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Pair lower a with upper b for the lower boundary.

- Pair upper a with lower b for the upper boundary and use strict inequalities.

## Practice 10 — extension · 2 marks

[GCSE Higher; IGCSE Extended] A result is known to satisfy 6.42≤y<6.46. Is its value to one decimal place guaranteed? Explain.

Answer: No. Values below 6.45 round to 6.4 and values at or above 6.45 round to 6.5 under the stated convention.

1. Locate the one-decimal rounding threshold 6.45.

2. The possible interval crosses that threshold.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify the threshold.

- Explain why both rounded outcomes remain possible.
