## Context selects a meaning, not just a familiar definition

Many words have more than one meaning, and a question asks for the sense supported by the particular passage. In the fictional sentence 'The committee tabled the proposal until the missing figures arrived', the action concerns a decision process, not furniture. Because usage can vary across varieties of English, a well-designed exercise supplies enough context to resolve the intended meaning. Your task is to use that evidence, not to insist that the first dictionary meaning you learned must fit every occurrence.

Separate the word's possible meanings from the author's claim. A synonym that is related in general may change what the sentence says. For example, describing an argument as qualified suggests limitations or conditions in a context about cautious claims; replacing it with certified could introduce an unsupported credentialing idea. Read the surrounding proposition in plain language before choosing a replacement. The best answer preserves the relevant relationship, degree and tone, not merely the part of speech or the general topic.

## Build a short prediction before comparing options

For a completion task, temporarily replace the gap with an uncomplicated paraphrase. In the fictional sentence 'Because the evidence covered only one site, Mara kept her conclusion ___ rather than claiming a universal pattern', a sensible prediction is limited or cautious. This does not require guessing the exact answer word. It establishes a semantic target against which options can be tested. A sophisticated-sounding choice is not better if it contradicts the reason supplied before the gap.

Identify why the writer included each clause. A cause introduced by because should support the result, whereas a contrast introduced by although can set up an unexpected relationship. A following example may define the intended sense more tightly than the opening topic does. If your prediction conflicts with a clear detail, revise the prediction rather than ignoring that detail. This is an evidence-driven revision, not an arbitrary change of guess. Keep the paraphrase brief enough that you can substitute it into the whole sentence and check whether the reasoning still works.

## Use contrast, restatement and degree as clues

Contrast clues often rule out an answer family. If an original fictional passage says a plan was 'tentative, not a final commitment', a word meaning irreversible is inconsistent with the explicit contrast. Restatement clues work differently: an appositive, explanation or example can clarify what the target word is doing. Punctuation helps locate those relationships, but punctuation alone does not determine a word's meaning. Read the actual propositions on both sides.

Degree matters even when two words point in the same direction. Evidence that a design reduced noise does not necessarily justify eliminated noise. A description of a possible explanation does not support an inevitable conclusion. Distractors often share a broad positive or negative association with the right answer but strengthen, weaken or redirect the claim. Compare the passage's stated level of certainty with the option's implied level. A careful answer preserves qualification unless the text supplies evidence for a stronger statement.

## Check grammatical fit without stopping at grammar

A completion must fit the sentence's syntax as well as its meaning. Identify whether the gap needs a noun, adjective, verb or adverb, and inspect any required preposition or grammatical relationship. 'The finding is consistent with the hypothesis' uses a different construction from 'The finding confirms the hypothesis'. Both can be grammatical in a suitable sentence, but the second may assert a stronger evidential relationship. Grammar can remove impossible choices, yet several grammatical options may remain.

Collocations are common word combinations, not automatic proof that a choice is correct. A familiar phrase can still contradict the evidence. Likewise, a rare word should not be rejected solely because it is unfamiliar if context and known word parts support it; however, word roots provide a clue rather than a guarantee. Substitute each surviving option and read the completed statement literally. Ask what new claim it would make and whether that claim is licensed by the passage. This final semantic check prevents a fluent phrase from hiding a wrong inference.

## Eliminate options with a stated reason

Useful elimination reasons are specific. An option may reverse the relationship, make the claim too strong, introduce an unrelated sense, fail the grammar or require a fact not supplied. Saying that an answer sounds wrong is less informative because it does not reveal which clue controls the decision. In an explanation, point to a short relevant phrase and connect it to the option's meaning. There is no need to invent a story about what the author secretly intended.

Consider the fictional sentence 'The researcher called the pattern anomalous because it departed from the otherwise consistent results'. Unusual or deviating from the pattern fits the explanation. Typical contradicts it, and fraudulent adds an unsupported allegation about wrongdoing. An anomaly is not automatically evidence of misconduct. This distinction shows why eliminating an option is more than finding a loose antonym. It requires preserving the evidence boundary of the passage, especially when a distractor imports a stronger causal, ethical or evaluative claim.

## Review errors by reasoning type, not word lists alone

After a practice item, record the contextual paraphrase, the decisive clue and why the selected distractor failed. Group errors by mechanism: familiar-meaning trap, missed contrast, excessive certainty, grammatical mismatch or unsupported implication. A vocabulary notebook can still help, but entries are more useful when they include a short original context and a distinction from a near-synonym. Memorising isolated substitutions encourages the mistaken belief that two words are interchangeable everywhere.

Practise with varied topics while remembering that the answer must be justified by the presented text. These fictional passages do not require outside knowledge about the named researcher, institution or project. If two options genuinely preserve every relevant feature, the item may need editorial repair rather than forced certainty. The present exercises include explanation tasks to make reasoning visible; they do not reproduce the full format or difficulty distribution of an official test. Wider reading, additional contextual senses and independently reviewed item quality remain necessary before any claim of complete skill coverage.

## Using a sentence-level contrast to select degree of meaning

A context question can turn on degree rather than a completely different dictionary sense. An adjective such as tentative may indicate that a conclusion is provisional, not that it is random or worthless. Use the surrounding qualification to predict the intended meaning before reviewing alternatives. Then substitute each candidate and check whether it preserves the sentence’s logic, intensity and grammatical role. A familiar synonym can still be too strong. Explain eliminations by pointing to a particular cue instead of saying that an option sounds wrong. ## Worked example 1

Fictional text: 'The director offered a provisional schedule, explaining that it could change after the venue confirmed its hours.' What does provisional mean?

1. The explanation explicitly allows later change.

2. Predict temporary or subject to revision.

3. Reject permanent because it contradicts the stated condition.

Answer: Tentative or subject to revision.

## Worked example 2

Fictional text: 'Far from obscuring the distinction, the new diagram made the two processes easier to tell apart.' What meaning of obscuring fits?

1. The sentence contrasts obscuring with easier discrimination.

2. Predict making unclear or harder to see.

3. Do not select merely drawing; the target describes an effect on clarity.

Answer: Making unclear.

## Worked example 3

Fictional text: 'The added measurements corroborated the earlier result, although the team still described its explanation as tentative.' Explain corroborated.

1. The measurements support an earlier result.

2. The qualification warns against turning support into final proof of every explanation.

Answer: Provided supporting evidence for.

## Worked example 4

Fictional completion: 'Since the change affected only one small detail, the reviewer described it as ___ rather than a comprehensive redesign.'

1. The cause limits the change's scale.

2. Predict limited or minor.

3. Reject sweeping because it conflicts with the explicit contrast.

Answer: Minor is a suitable completion.

## Worked example 5

Fictional text: 'The historian qualified the claim by restricting it to the surviving letters.' Which sense of qualified is intended?

1. The phrase 'by restricting it' explains the action.

2. The target concerns the scope of a claim, not someone's credentials.

Answer: Limited or modified with a condition.

## Worked example 6

Original sentence: “The team offered a tentative explanation, stressing that the small sample required further testing.” In context, does tentative most nearly mean provisional, careless or inevitable? Explain.

1. The sentence explicitly limits the evidence through a small sample.

2. Further testing implies the explanation is not settled.

3. Careless adds a judgement about effort and inevitable adds certainty, neither supported by the cue.

Answer: Provisional. The need for further testing makes the explanation subject to revision.

## Misconceptions

- The most familiar meaning of a word must be correct.
  Correction: The passage selects the relevant sense from the word's possible meanings.

- Two grammatical options must be equally good.
  Correction: They may differ in logical relationship, degree or unsupported implications.

- An unusual finding proves misconduct.
  Correction: An anomaly indicates a departure from a pattern; wrongdoing needs separate evidence.
