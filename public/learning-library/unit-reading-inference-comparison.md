## Original practice passage A: a traveller

At seven, the station clock still insisted it was six. Mara stood beneath it with a suitcase that would not close and a ticket softened by rain. Beyond the glass, buses came and went in bright, indifferent lines. She had rehearsed a confident goodbye, but now her phone rested silent in her palm. When the platform number finally appeared, she checked it twice. Then she lifted the case, leaving a small dark square on the tiles where it had stood.

## Original practice passage B: a station notice

The station will introduce a staffed information desk beside the main entrance next month. A passenger survey identified difficulty finding platform changes and assistance during disrupted journeys. The desk will operate from six in the morning until ten at night. Large-print timetables will be available, while existing electronic displays will remain in use. The manager says the changes will make journeys simpler. However, the notice does not explain what assistance will be available overnight or how passengers who do not enter through the main doors will locate the desk.

## Evidence before inference

Explicit meaning is stated directly: Mara checks the platform number twice. An inference connects that detail with a plausible idea, such as uncertainty or a desire for reassurance. It does not justify inventing a missed train, a family argument or a destination. Select the smallest useful detail and explain the connection. In passage B, distinguish a planned change from an established result: the manager’s prediction does not prove that journeys have already become simpler.

## Language and sequence work together

The clock that insists personifies a faulty object, creating tension between official certainty and the traveller’s experience. The description of buses as indifferent gives the setting an emotional distance. Do not stop at naming personification: explain how it relates to Mara’s isolation. Structurally, passage A moves from stalled waiting to a small decisive action. The final trace on the tiles can emphasise departure without claiming a unique mandatory interpretation. An effect must be argued from the wording and sequence, not from a memorised list of devices.

## Comparison, summary and purposeful writing

Compare both passages through a shared question. A presents uncertainty as an individual experience using close detail; B presents an institutional response through proposed services and practical limits. A summary of the notice’s changes should select the desk, hours and accessible timetables, not analyse the traveller’s suitcase. An evaluation should separate benefits from unresolved questions. When adapting material into a letter, speech or report, preserve facts while choosing an appropriate voice and structure. Do not add invented survey percentages to sound persuasive. Proofread sentence boundaries and references so the reader can follow who does what.

## Worked example 1

Make an evidence-based inference about Mara checking the number twice.

1. Start with the stated repeated action.

2. Offer a cautious inference without inventing a specific reason for travel.

Answer: The repetition suggests uncertainty or a wish for reassurance before departure.

## Worked example 2

Compare how the passages present the need for information.

1. Identify a shared concern.

2. Use evidence from each text and explain the difference in purpose.

Answer: A dramatises one traveller’s uncertainty; B proposes an organised service to answer a surveyed need while leaving some access questions unresolved.

## Misconceptions

- Any plausible backstory is a valid inference.
  Correction: An inference must be constrained by details in the text.

- Naming a device explains its effect.
  Correction: Analysis connects the choice to meaning, purpose and the reader’s experience.

## Flashcards

### What is explicit meaning?
Information directly stated.

### What makes an inference defensible?
A clear connection to specific textual evidence.

### What does structural analysis examine?
The ordering and changing focus of material.

### What makes comparison more than two summaries?
An explicit relationship organised around a shared issue.

### Why separate a promise from a result?
A prediction is not evidence that the outcome has already occurred.

## Practice 2 — standard · 3 marks

[GCSE/IGCSE: untiered] Infer Mara’s state of mind from her rehearsed goodbye and silent phone, supporting your reading.

Answer: She may feel hesitant or isolated despite intending to appear confident.

1. Contrast the rehearsed confidence with the silent present moment.

2. Keep the conclusion tentative; no specific relationship conflict is stated.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Plausible inference.

- Precise evidence.

- No unsupported backstory.

## Practice 3 — standard · 3 marks

[GCSE/IGCSE: untiered] Explain the effect of presenting the clock as insisting on the wrong time.

Answer: Personification gives a faulty official object stubborn certainty, contrasting with the traveller’s uncertain experience.

1. Identify the human action applied to the clock.

2. Connect the mismatch to the situation rather than merely naming personification.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Method recognised.

- Mismatch explained.

- Contextual effect.

## Practice 4 — standard · 4 marks

[GCSE/IGCSE: untiered] In no more than 45 words, summarise the changes announced in passage B.

Answer: A staffed information desk will open beside the main entrance next month, operating from 6 am to 10 pm. Large-print timetables will be available, and electronic displays will remain.

1. Select the announced provisions.

2. Use concise own wording without adding an evaluation or invented statistics.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Desk location and timing.

- Operating hours.

- Timetables and retained displays.

## Practice 5 — standard · 3 marks

[GCSE/IGCSE: untiered] Compare how A and B present travellers’ uncertainty.

Answer: A conveys a personal emotional experience; B identifies an organisational problem and proposed practical response.

1. Use a detail from A and a provision or limitation from B.

2. Explain how different purposes influence presentation.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- A evidence.

- B evidence.

- Explicit comparison.

## Practice 6 — standard · 3 marks

[GCSE/IGCSE: untiered] Write a short paragraph to the station manager supporting one proposal and asking for clarification of one unresolved issue.

Answer: For example: The staffed desk should help passengers respond to platform changes. Please explain how assistance will be provided after 10 pm, when the desk is closed.

1. Maintain a suitable direct, courteous voice.

2. Link support and the question to accurate information from the notice.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Relevant benefit.

- Accurate unresolved question.

- Appropriate audience and clear sentences.
