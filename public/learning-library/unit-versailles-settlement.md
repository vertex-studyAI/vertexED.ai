## A peace negotiated between different priorities

Wilson promoted a settlement associated with his Fourteen Points, national self-determination and a League of Nations. Clemenceau sought French security after invasion and destruction, favouring constraints on German power. Lloyd George faced British pressure to punish Germany but also concerns about trade and a European balance of power. These aims overlapped in preventing renewed aggression but differed over how punishment, economic recovery and security should be balanced.

Self-determination was not applied consistently to every population. Economic, strategic and imperial considerations competed with it. A strong explanation of compromise identifies both the desired policy and the obstacle: one leader’s failure to secure everything does not mean that leader achieved nothing.

## Terms and the distinction between 1919 and 1921

Germany lost territory, including Alsace-Lorraine to France, and lost its overseas colonies, which became mandates. The army was limited to 100000 men; conscription, an air force and submarines were prohibited, and the Rhineland was demilitarised. Article 231 established a basis for responsibility for losses and reparations and became a focus of German resentment. The final reparations total was fixed in 1921, not printed as a settled sum in the June 1919 treaty. Treaty details must therefore be placed in their correct chronological setting.

## Consequences were mediated by decisions

Many Germans condemned the treaty as imposed because they had little effective role in negotiating it. Territorial losses and military restrictions challenged national pride, while reparations contributed to financial pressure. However, explaining economic breakdown requires more than naming reparations. Occupation of the Ruhr in 1923, passive resistance, lost output and government finance linked obligations and policy choices to worsening inflation. Earlier war debts and fiscal weaknesses also mattered. The treaty’s political impact included attacks on leaders associated with accepting the settlement.

## How to judge fairness and sources

Fairness is a judgement requiring criteria: security for invaded states, treatment of civilians, consistency of self-determination and prospects for lasting peace may lead to different assessments. A French security argument should be evaluated against France’s experience of invasion; a German protest against exclusion should be tested against the negotiation process and actual terms. For a cartoon or speech, identify the message, intended audience and purpose before considering reliability for a particular question. A partisan source may be valuable evidence of opinion even when weak evidence for an objective economic prediction. Do not treat the outbreak of war in 1939 as proving that every development from 1919 was inevitable.

## Worked example 1

Why might Britain support German economic recovery while accepting punishment?

1. Separate economic interests from the pressure for retribution.

2. Explain why these pressures could produce compromise rather than a single consistent maximum punishment.

Answer: Trade and stability could benefit Britain, while public opinion and security encouraged penalties.

## Worked example 2

An original practice speech says: Our border must never again be crossed by an invading army. How could this help analyse a French argument for restrictions?

1. Connect the stated fear to invasion and proposed limits on German strength.

2. Separate understanding an argument from accepting all its conclusions.

Answer: It illustrates a security justification, but is not an authentic source or proof that every restriction was proportionate.

## Misconceptions

- The Big Three had identical aims.
  Correction: They shared some concerns but differed over punishment, security, trade and international cooperation.

- All reparations details were fixed in June 1919.
  Correction: The final total was set in 1921; distinguish the treaty obligation from its later implementation.

## Practice 3 — standard · 2 marks

[IGCSE: untiered] Why should a timeline distinguish the June 1919 treaty from the reparations total fixed in 1921?

Answer: The treaty imposed an obligation before the final sum was set.

1. Separate agreement in principle from later implementation.

2. Avoid assigning a later decision to the earlier document.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Obligation versus sum.

- Correct chronology.

## Practice 4 — standard · 3 marks

[IGCSE: untiered] Give one argument that the settlement was justified and one that it was unfair, then identify a criterion for weighing them.

Answer: Security and compensation for invaded states support it; exclusion and inconsistent self-determination challenge it. A judgement can weigh security against proportionality.

1. Use specific features rather than just feelings.

2. State a criterion and explain its trade-off.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Justification with detail.

- Criticism with detail.

- Explicit criterion.
