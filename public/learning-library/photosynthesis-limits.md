## Matter and energy in the reaction

Photosynthesis builds organic molecules using carbon dioxide and water, with light supplying energy captured by photosynthetic pigments. A school-level overall summary is 6CO₂+6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆+6O₂ in the presence of light. This compact equation balances atoms, but it is not a claim that the whole process occurs in a single chemical step. Carbon atoms in the carbohydrate come from carbon dioxide, not from light. Light transfers energy; it does not supply the carbon mass.

Chloroplasts contain the machinery for photosynthesis. Calling the overall process endothermic describes its need for energy input from the surroundings. Plants also respire, including when illuminated: photosynthesis does not replace the cell's energy-releasing reactions. A measured oxygen release can therefore represent net gas exchange rather than the gross rate of oxygen production. Distinguish the simplified reaction model from the measurement used to investigate it.

## What limits a rate

A limiting factor is a condition whose shortage or restriction constrains the rate under the circumstances being considered. Increasing light intensity can increase photosynthesis when light is limiting, but the response may level off when another factor prevents further increase. Carbon dioxide availability, temperature and chlorophyll content can all matter. A flat section of a rate-versus-light graph does not prove that photosynthesis has stopped; it indicates little additional rate response to more light in that region.

Temperature affects enzyme-mediated processes. Raising temperature from a low value may increase rate, while sufficiently high temperatures can impair enzyme function and other cellular processes. Do not infer that the hottest condition must give the fastest sustained rate. The effect of one factor depends on the others. Identifying interactions among several factors is Higher-tier reasoning in the mapped specification, rather than an unqualified demand on every tier.

## Comparing curves and testing explanations

When two rate-light curves differ, first identify what changed between the experiments and what was controlled. If increasing carbon dioxide raises the plateau while other conditions remain comparable, that supports the interpretation that carbon dioxide constrained the earlier plateau. It does not prove that carbon dioxide is the only limitation at every light level. Near the low-light end, the curves may still be limited mainly by light.

Graphs provide evidence within their measured range. A smooth line joining a handful of points is not proof that every intermediate or later condition behaves exactly as drawn. Use axis units, repeated observations and variability to judge a difference. A small apparent change may fall within measurement uncertainty. A good explanation links a proposed mechanism to a particular region of the graph instead of merely listing every possible factor.

## Rates, intensity and inverse-square assumptions

A rate is a measured change divided by elapsed time. For collected oxygen volume, use volume per minute or another stated unit. Convert the time interval consistently and subtract any initial reading where required. Counting bubbles is a convenient proxy, but bubbles need not have equal volumes; a bubble count is not automatically an accurate oxygen-volume rate. Repeating measurements can reveal variability without eliminating every systematic error.

For an ideal point-like light source in suitable geometry, intensity varies approximately as 1/d². Doubling distance therefore reduces this idealised intensity to one quarter, not one half. This inverse-square treatment is a Higher-tier component of the mapped AQA topic. Real lamps, reflections and ambient light may violate the simple model, especially at small separations. Distance is a manipulated variable or proxy; a sensor can measure the actual illumination more directly.

## Design and interpretation of an investigation

An aquatic-organism investigation can compare net oxygen output at several light conditions while controlling relevant variables such as temperature, organism size and carbon dioxide availability. Allow time for the system to adjust before measuring, keep the collection period consistent, and use repeated observations. If a lamp also warms the water, a distance change may affect two variables simultaneously. The interpretation must then account for that confounding influence.

This is planning guidance, not a stand-alone laboratory protocol. Follow the school risk assessment and supervision requirements for water, electrical equipment, glassware and living material. Do not treat completion of an online worksheet as practical competence. Evaluate the measure itself as well as the procedure: gas collection, bubble counts and changes in dissolved oxygen have different limitations, and respiration can affect the net result.

## Uses of products and syllabus limits

Photosynthetic products supply carbon compounds for growth and storage. Glucose can be used in respiration, converted into storage carbohydrates or incorporated into structural and other organic molecules. Producing amino acids also requires a nitrogen source, commonly supplied through absorbed mineral ions; carbon dioxide, water and light alone do not provide all the elements needed to build proteins. A starch store can accumulate when production exceeds immediate use.

## Distinguishing gross production from net gas exchange

A gas-exchange measurement can reflect more than photosynthesis alone. In a simplified model, oxygen production by photosynthesis and oxygen use in respiration occur during the same interval. The net measured oxygen gain is their difference. Zero net gain therefore does not imply that both processes have stopped; their rates may balance. A rate change after increasing light suggests an effect of light under those conditions, but a plateau does not by itself identify the next limiting factor. Use additional controlled comparisons to test alternatives. The numerical example is an invented steady-rate model, and assumes no other gas exchanges or storage effects contribute to the measurement.

## Explain interacting limits using controlled comparisons

A plateau in a rate graph says that increasing the tested factor no longer increases the measured response under those conditions. It does not identify the limiting factor without additional comparisons. Change one candidate factor while controlling others and observe whether the plateau moves. When using net oxygen output, remember that respiration occurs alongside photosynthesis. Light intensity, temperature, carbon dioxide supply and the measurement method can each affect the result. Repeated readings help assess variability, while a controlled design addresses confounding; these are complementary tasks.

## Worked example 1

An organism releases 4.8 cm³ of collected gas in four minutes. Find the measured volume rate.

1. Divide volume by time.

2. Compute 4.8/4.

3. Retain the units and call this a measured gas-output rate.

Answer: 1.2 cm³/min.

## Worked example 2

Under an ideal inverse-square lamp model, distance changes from 15 cm to 30 cm. Find the new intensity as a fraction of the old.

1. Use Inew/Iold=(dold/dnew)².

2. Calculate (15/30)².

3. This refers to intensity, not automatically the same fractional change in photosynthesis.

Answer: 1/4 of the original intensity.

## Worked example 3

A rate-light curve plateaus. Increasing carbon dioxide raises the plateau. Interpret this evidence.

1. More light alone had little effect in the plateau region.

2. The response to extra carbon dioxide supports a carbon-dioxide limitation there.

3. Other conditions and measurement uncertainty still need consideration.

Answer: Carbon dioxide likely constrained the earlier plateau under the tested conditions.

## Worked example 4

Two bubble counts are equal, but one trial has visibly larger bubbles. Can equal oxygen-volume output be concluded?

1. Counts measure numbers of bubbles.

2. Gas volume also depends on bubble size.

3. Use volume measurement or acknowledge the proxy limitation.

Answer: No; equal counts do not establish equal gas volumes.

## Worked example 5

In a fictional model, photosynthesis produces 9 oxygen units per minute while respiration uses 4. Find net oxygen gain over 6 minutes, assuming constant rates and no other exchanges.

1. Net rate is production minus use: 9 - 4 = 5 units per minute.

2. The interval is 6 minutes with the rate held constant.

3. Multiply 5 × 6 to obtain a net gain of 30 units.

Answer: Net gain is 30 oxygen units.

## Misconceptions

- Light supplies the atoms in glucose.
  Correction: Light supplies energy; the carbon is supplied by carbon dioxide.

- A plateau means no photosynthesis occurs.
  Correction: It means increasing the plotted input no longer raises the observed rate appreciably.

- Plants only respire in darkness.
  Correction: Respiration also occurs in illuminated living plant cells.

## Flashcards

### What supplies the carbon in photosynthetic carbohydrate?
Carbon dioxide.

### What does light supply to photosynthesis?
Energy, not the carbon atoms.

### What does limiting factor mean in a particular experiment?
A condition that constrains the rate under those tested circumstances.

### What does a plateau in rate versus light indicate?
Little further rate response to more light, not necessarily a zero rate.

### Why can excessive temperature reduce photosynthetic rate?
It can impair enzymes and other cellular processes.

### How is a gas-output rate calculated?
Measured gas change divided by elapsed time, with consistent units.

### Under the ideal inverse-square model, what happens when lamp distance doubles?
Intensity becomes one quarter.

### Why are bubble counts an imperfect volume proxy?
Bubble volumes may differ.

### Why can measured oxygen output differ from gross photosynthesis?
Respiration and other gas-exchange effects can alter the net output.

### Why are mineral nitrogen sources needed when plants make protein?
Amino acids contain nitrogen, which is not supplied by carbon dioxide and water alone.

### What is net oxygen gain in the simplified two-process model?
Oxygen produced by photosynthesis minus oxygen used in respiration.

### What can zero net gas exchange conceal?
Two nonzero processes operating at equal opposing rates.
