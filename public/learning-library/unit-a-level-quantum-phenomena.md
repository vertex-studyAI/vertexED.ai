## One photon, one energy transfer

A photon has energy E = hf. In the simple photoelectric model one photon transfers its energy to one electron. The work function is the minimum energy needed for an electron to escape the surface. If hf is too small, increasing intensity does not make individual photons energetic enough. The threshold frequency is f0 = work function divided by h. Above threshold, raising intensity at fixed frequency increases the photon arrival rate and can increase emitted electron rate, not their maximum kinetic energy.

## Energy accounting and stopping potential

For the fastest emitted electrons, hf = work function + maximum kinetic energy. Some electrons lose more energy before escape, so the equation gives a maximum rather than the energy of every electron. A stopping potential of magnitude Vs removes kinetic energy eVs from an electron, giving Emax = eVs. An electron volt is an energy unit: 1 eV = 1.60 × 10^-19 J for calculations here. Do not substitute a work function in eV alongside hf in joules without conversion.

## Excitation, ionisation and spectra

An atom can absorb energy so that an electron moves to a higher allowed energy level: excitation. Ionisation removes an electron from the atom entirely. When an excited electron drops to a lower allowed level, the emitted photon carries the difference in energy. Discrete differences give discrete spectral lines rather than every possible frequency. In a fluorescent tube, accelerated electrons excite gas atoms; the resulting radiation can excite a coating that emits visible light. A transition from -2 eV to -5 eV emits 3 eV, not -3 eV.

## Matter waves and model limits

Electron diffraction produces patterns that require wave behaviour. The de Broglie wavelength is lambda = h/p; in a non-relativistic example p = mv. Increasing momentum decreases wavelength, so diffraction angles for a fixed crystal spacing decrease. Photoelectric emission supports a particulate account of light, while diffraction supports wave properties of matter. These are evidence-led models for different observations, not proof that an electron alternates between an ordinary tiny ball and an ordinary water wave. Always state which observation your explanation addresses.

## Worked example 1

A photon has energy 4.8 eV and the metal work function is 2.0 eV. Find maximum kinetic energy and stopping potential.

1. Subtract the work function: 4.8 - 2.0 = 2.8 eV.

2. An electron loses 2.8 eV across an opposing potential difference of 2.8 V.

Answer: 2.8 eV; 2.8 V.

## Worked example 2

An electron transition releases 3.0 eV. Find frequency using e=1.60 × 10^-19 C and h=6.63 × 10^-34 J s.

1. Convert energy to 4.80 × 10^-19 J.

2. Divide by h; the energy difference is positive for the emitted photon.

Answer: Approximately 7.24 × 10^14 Hz.

## Misconceptions

- Brighter low-frequency light must eventually eject electrons.
  Correction: Below threshold, individual photons lack the required energy in this model.

- An atomic emission photon has negative energy because the electron loses energy.
  Correction: The electron’s change is negative; the emitted photon carries a positive energy equal to the level difference.

## Flashcards

### What is photon energy?
E = hf.

### What sets threshold frequency?
The surface work function divided by Planck’s constant.

### What does higher intensity change at fixed frequency?
Photon rate, not photon energy.

### What causes a line spectrum?
Transitions between discrete allowed energies.

### How does momentum affect de Broglie wavelength?
Wavelength is inversely proportional to momentum.

[A-Level: untiered] A metal does not emit photoelectrons at a particular frequency. Explain why doubling intensity may still produce none.

Answer: The photon energy can remain below the work function.

1. Frequency and hence hf remain fixed.

2. More insufficient-energy photons do not satisfy the one-photon threshold.

## Practice 2 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Photons of 5.2 eV strike a surface with work function 2.3 eV. Find maximum kinetic energy and stopping-potential magnitude.

Answer: 2.9 eV and 2.9 V.

1. Subtract 2.3 from 5.2.

2. Use Emax = eVs.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- 2.9 eV.

- 2.9 V.

## Practice 3 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Find threshold frequency for work function 3.315 × 10^-19 J with h=6.63 × 10^-34 J s.

Answer: 5.00 × 10^14 Hz.

1. At threshold hf0 equals work function.

2. Divide 3.315 × 10^-19 by 6.63 × 10^-34.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Correct equation.

- 5.00 × 10^14 Hz.

## Practice 4 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] An atomic electron drops from -1.5 eV to -4.0 eV. State the emitted photon energy and distinguish this from ionisation.

Answer: 2.5 eV; the electron remains bound rather than leaving the atom.

1. Subtract lower energy from higher energy.

2. Ionisation requires removal, not a transition between two bound levels.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- 2.5 eV.

- Bound transition contrasted with removal.

## Practice 5 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] An electron’s non-relativistic momentum doubles. Predict its de Broglie wavelength and the change in diffraction angle at fixed crystal spacing.

Answer: Wavelength halves; diffraction angle decreases.

1. Use lambda = h/p.

2. A shorter wavelength gives smaller diffraction angles for the same order and spacing.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Wavelength halves.

- Angle decreases.

## Practice 6 — extension · 3 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Above threshold, compare raising intensity at fixed frequency with raising frequency at fixed intensity. Focus on maximum photoelectron energy.

Answer: Intensity alone leaves maximum energy unchanged; higher frequency increases it.

1. Emax = hf - work function contains frequency but not intensity.

2. Intensity changes the available photon rate; photon energy rises with frequency.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Intensity distinction.

- Frequency distinction.

- Equation supports reasoning.
