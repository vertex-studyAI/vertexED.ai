## Scarcity and allocation: IGCSE and A-Level

Resources are limited relative to competing wants, so decisions must be made about what to produce, how to produce it and who receives the output. Scarcity is not identical to poverty: a wealthy economy still faces limits on labour time, land and environmental capacity. Economic goods use scarce resources and have opportunity costs even if no price is charged to the user. A publicly funded lesson uses teacher time and buildings; free at the point of use does not mean costless to society.

## Opportunity cost and a two-good model

A production possibility curve describes combinations of two outputs that are feasible at a given time with available resources and technology used efficiently. Suppose a fictional economy can produce (0 food, 12 machines), (4 food, 10 machines), (7 food, 6 machines) or (9 food, 0 machines). Moving from the second to the third point gains 3 food units while giving up 4 machines. The total opportunity cost is 4 machines; per extra food unit it is 4/3 machines. State what the units measure.

## Movement, unused resources and growth

A point inside the frontier may indicate unemployed resources or inefficient use of them. Moving towards the existing frontier uses capacity more fully; it is not the same as an outward shift in productive capacity. More or better resources and improved technology may shift the frontier outward. Damage to productive resources can shift it inward. A point beyond the current frontier is currently unattainable within the model, not impossible forever. Uneven technological improvement can increase one intercept more than the other, so not every growth shift is parallel.

## Efficiency and judgement: A-Level extension

A point on the frontier is productively efficient because more of one output cannot be produced without sacrificing another, under the model’s assumptions. It does not follow that the mix is allocatively efficient or fair. Those judgements concern how output matches preferences and social costs and benefits. An economy could efficiently produce too many machines relative to what its population values. A bowed frontier can represent increasing opportunity cost because resources are not equally suited to both uses. A straight line represents constant opportunity cost, an assumption rather than a universal law. Keep normative claims about what should be produced distinct from positive claims that can be tested against evidence.

## Worked example 1

Using the fictional frontier, find the opportunity cost of moving from (4 food,10 machines) to (7 food,6 machines).

1. Calculate the changes separately: +3 food, -4 machines.

2. Express cost in the forgone output, not the output gained.

Answer: 4 machines in total, or 4/3 machines per extra food unit.

## Worked example 2

A-Level: does producing on a frontier prove the economy has chosen the best mix?

1. The frontier limits technically feasible combinations.

2. Preferences and social costs/benefits are needed to judge the mix.

Answer: No; productive efficiency does not establish allocative efficiency or fairness.

## Misconceptions

- A zero price means no opportunity cost.
  Correction: Scarce resources can be used even when the consumer pays no direct charge.

- Any rise in output shifts the frontier.
  Correction: Using unemployed resources can move production towards an unchanged frontier.

## Flashcards

### What creates scarcity?
Limited resources relative to competing wants.

### What is opportunity cost?
The next best alternative forgone.

### What can an interior point represent?
Unused resources or inefficient use.

### What shifts a frontier outward?
Increased productive capacity through resources or technology.

### Does productive efficiency prove allocative efficiency?
No; the chosen mix still needs evaluation against preferences and social costs/benefits.

[IGCSE: untiered] A public library charges no entry fee. Explain why its service is still an economic good.

Answer: It uses scarce staff time, buildings and other resources with alternative uses.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

[IGCSE: untiered] A frontier move raises food from 5 to 8 units while machines fall from 14 to 8. Calculate total and per-unit opportunity cost.

Answer: 6 machines total; 2 machines per additional food unit.

1. Food rises by 3 and machines fall by 6.

2. 6 / 3 = 2.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- 6 machines.

- 2 machines per food unit.

## Practice 3 — standard · 2 marks

[IGCSE: untiered] Unemployed workers obtain jobs using existing idle factories. Explain whether this necessarily shifts the production frontier.

Answer: It can move output towards the existing frontier without increasing maximum capacity.

1. Identify formerly unused resources.

2. Distinguish actual production from potential production.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Movement towards frontier.

- No necessary capacity increase.

## Practice 4 — standard · 2 marks

[IGCSE: untiered] A new technique improves only machine production. Explain why the frontier need not shift outward equally for both outputs.

Answer: Machine capacity may rise more while maximum food output remains unchanged.

1. Link technology to the affected activity.

2. Describe an uneven outward shift rather than assuming parallel movement.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Machine capacity link.

- Uneven shift.

## Practice 5 — extension · 3 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Why can a point on a production frontier be productively efficient but allocatively inefficient?

Answer: It uses resources efficiently but may produce a mix not matching social preferences and costs/benefits.

1. Define the technical trade-off on the frontier.

2. Distinguish the desirability of the selected combination.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Productive definition.

- Mix judgement.

## Practice 6 — extension · 3 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Explain why a bowed production frontier can show rising opportunity cost as more resources move into one activity.

Answer: Resources increasingly transferred may be less suited to the expanding activity and more valuable in the activity reduced.

1. Explain resource heterogeneity.

2. Link it to more forgone output per additional unit.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Different resource suitability.

- Rising sacrifice.
