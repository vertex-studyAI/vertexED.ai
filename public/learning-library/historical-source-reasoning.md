# Provenance, corroboration and historical explanations

Subject: History · Unit: Historical enquiry · Revision: 2026-10-21.3

**Review draft — not independently approved. Practice marks are editorial, not official exam marks.**

## Prerequisites

- Distinguish an event from an account of it.

- Identify a claim and supporting detail.

## Objectives

- Evaluate a source relative to a precise historical question.

- Use provenance and context without blanket reliability labels.

- Corroborate claims and distinguish causes from sequences.

## Usefulness depends on the question

A historical source is evidence created in or about a past context. Its usefulness depends on what you want to learn. A public speech may be strong evidence of the argument a leader wanted an audience to hear, but weaker evidence of private motives or actual public agreement. Begin with a precise question, then identify what the source directly reveals and what it cannot establish. Primary does not automatically mean accurate, and later does not automatically mean useless. Later scholarship may compare many records unavailable to an eyewitness, while also being shaped by its own questions and methods.

## Provenance and corroboration

Provenance includes who created the source, when, for whom and for what purpose. These features suggest opportunities and constraints, not an automatic verdict that the source is reliable or biased in every respect. Ask whether the creator had access to the relevant information, incentives to present it selectively, or reasons to use a particular language or form. Corroboration compares a claim with other evidence. Several accounts repeating the same original report are not fully independent confirmation. Agreement can strengthen confidence when sources have independent access, while disagreement may reveal different perspectives, dates, definitions or errors. Absence of a claim in one surviving record is not necessarily proof that an event never occurred.

## Explaining change and causation

A historical explanation connects conditions, decisions and events through a reasoned mechanism. Events occurring in sequence do not automatically form a causal chain. Separate longer-term conditions from immediate triggers, and consider why an outcome occurred at that time rather than simply listing everything that preceded it. Weigh the relative importance of factors using evidence and acknowledge contingency: people acted with incomplete knowledge, and alternatives may have been possible. Avoid judging past actors solely through information available later. A strong conclusion answers the original question, identifies the best-supported explanation and makes uncertainty explicit rather than claiming more precision than the surviving evidence permits.

## Corroboration, silence and a defensible judgement

Corroboration is stronger when sources provide meaningfully independent evidence. Two newspapers repeating the same agency report do not necessarily count as two independent observations. Trace how information could have passed between sources and compare their purposes, access and timing. Agreement may support a claim, while disagreement can reveal different experiences, definitions or interests rather than simply identifying one source as worthless.

Absence of a reference is not automatically evidence that an event did not happen. Ask whether the source was expected to record it, whether the record survives, and whether the author had an opportunity or reason to mention it. A private letter and a government statistical return have different expected contents. In an evaluation, connect each limitation to the specific historical question. A source may be weak evidence for the numerical scale of an event but valuable evidence for contemporary attitudes toward it. Build a judgement from what the source supports, what other evidence adds and what remains uncertain. Avoid treating origin, purpose and value as isolated checklist words without explaining their consequences.

## Detecting dependence between apparently corroborating sources

Two accounts agreeing is stronger corroboration when their agreement comes from independent access to evidence. If a later newspaper copies an earlier report, the two texts may repeat one observation rather than supply two independent observations. Trace dates, wording, attribution and possible shared informants before counting sources. Dependence does not make the later account useless: it may reveal how a story circulated or was framed for a different audience. Usefulness therefore remains tied to the historical question. A source can be weak evidence for an event’s exact size yet useful evidence for contemporary publicity. The documents described here are invented teaching examples.

## Define what independent corroboration would establish

A source's value depends on the historical question being asked. A campaign speech can be strong evidence of public messaging while being weak evidence that its promises were fulfilled. A later copy can reveal transmission or reception even if it adds no independent witness. Check dates, audience, purpose and access to information before comparing claims. Numerical disagreement needs investigation of definitions and counting methods, not an automatic preference for the larger or earlier figure. Claims about causes usually require more than chronological order and should address plausible alternative explanations.

## Worked example 1

Invented scenario: a factory owner's public notice says working conditions are excellent. What can it establish?

1. Identify the creator and the public audience.

2. The notice directly evidences the owner's public representation of conditions.

3. Worker accounts, inspection records or other evidence are needed to evaluate actual conditions.

Answer: Useful for studying the owner's public message; insufficient alone to establish workplace conditions.

## Worked example 2

Invented scenario: three newspapers print identical wording from one agency report. Are these three independent eyewitness confirmations?

1. Trace the common origin of the wording.

2. Separate publication count from independent information sources.

3. Seek evidence with a genuinely separate basis.

Answer: No. They may represent one underlying report reproduced three times.

## Worked example 3

Two accounts use identical wording about a protest. What should be checked before treating their agreement as independent corroboration?

1. Consider whether both copied a common report.

2. Investigate authorship, publication sequence and access to the event.

3. Distinguish repetition of one claim from separate observations supporting it.

Answer: Check independence and transmission before counting the accounts as separate corroboration.

## Worked example 4

A fictional diary says 200 people attended a meeting. A later newspaper repeats the same number and explicitly cites the diary. How much independent corroboration does the newspaper supply for attendance, and for what other question could it be useful?

1. The newspaper identifies the diary as its source for the number.

2. The repeated figure therefore traces to one stated observation.

3. Publication still provides evidence about circulation or presentation of the account.

Answer: It supplies no independent attendance observation in the stated scenario, but it can show how the diary’s account circulated publicly.

## Misconceptions

- A biased source is useless.
  Correction: Its perspective may itself be valuable evidence for an appropriate question.

- An event happened first, so it caused the later event.
  Correction: Chronology is necessary for many causal claims but does not establish the mechanism or exclude alternatives.

## Flashcards

### What questions help establish provenance?
Who created the source, when, for whom and for what purpose?

### What must be specified before judging a source's usefulness?
The historical question being investigated.

### Why can three agreeing sources provide only one line of evidence?
They may all copy or depend on the same original account.

### Why avoid hindsight when explaining decisions?
Actors did not necessarily possess later knowledge of outcomes.

### What distinguishes a causal explanation from a timeline?
It explains a mechanism linking factors to an outcome and evaluates alternatives.

### Why can a source be useful for attitudes but weak for event totals?
Its author may directly reveal a viewpoint without having reliable access to complete numerical evidence.

### Why check whether sources depend on each other?
Agreement may repeat one underlying report rather than independently corroborate it.

### Can a dependent source remain useful?
Yes, for questions such as circulation, framing or reception, depending on its provenance and content.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

In an invented scenario, a ruler commissions a victory monument. Give one useful historical question and one claim it cannot establish alone.

Answer: It can help investigate how the ruler wished the victory to be represented. It cannot alone prove every claim about casualties, motives or popular support.

1. Link the public commission to representation and audience.

2. Identify a factual or social claim requiring corroboration.

## Practice 3 — standard · 2 marks

Why should a historian check whether apparently agreeing reports share an original source?

Answer: Shared dependence can make agreement less independent than it appears, so the number of reports may overstate the number of separate observations.

1. Identify possible copying or common sourcing.

2. Explain how this affects the strength of corroboration.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify source dependence.

- Explain its evidential consequence.

## Practice 4 — extension · 3 marks

Explain how to evaluate the claim that a protest caused a policy change when the change followed the protest but officials had already begun reviewing the policy.

Answer: Establish the chronology, investigate how the protest influenced decision-makers, and compare the existing review process as an alternative or interacting cause. Timing alone does not establish the protest as the sole cause.

1. Separate sequence from causal mechanism.

2. Use decision records or other relevant evidence to investigate influence.

3. Consider the pre-existing review and possible interaction between factors.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Reject chronology alone as proof.

- Identify evidence relevant to a mechanism.

- Evaluate the alternative or interacting cause.

## Practice 5 — extension · 3 marks

A diary does not mention a local flood. Give two questions needed before concluding that no flood occurred.

Answer: Ask whether the diarist was present and normally recorded local weather or disruptions, and whether the relevant pages survive. Silence is more informative when the event would probably have been noticed and recorded.

1. Examine the author's opportunity to observe the event.

2. Examine whether the source's purpose and normal practice made a mention likely.

3. Consider survival or completeness before interpreting silence.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Examine the author's opportunity to observe the event.

- Examine whether the source's purpose and normal practice made a mention likely.

- Consider survival or completeness before interpreting silence.

A learner dismisses both texts as worthless because one copied the other. Explain why this judgement is too broad and name a way to investigate attendance further.

## Practice 8 — standard · 3 marks

In a fictional town, a candidate promises in a speech to build three bridges. A historian cites the speech as proof that three bridges were completed. Evaluate the use and name better outcome evidence.

Answer: The speech supports a claim about the promise, not completion. Construction records, dated maps or inspected surviving structures could address completion.

1. Separate an intended future action from evidence that it occurred.

2. Match the proposed evidence to the outcome question.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify the promise as supported.

- Reject completion as established by the speech.

- Name relevant completion evidence.

## Practice 9 — extension · 3 marks

Two independent fictional reports give 300 and 450 people at a rally. One counted at noon; the other counted everyone who entered over three hours. Explain why the figures need not directly contradict each other.

Answer: They measure different populations: attendance at one moment versus cumulative entrants. People may arrive and leave, and repeat entry may also affect the cumulative count.

1. Compare time windows and counting definitions.

2. Investigate whether entrants were unique before reconciling totals.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Distinguish snapshot and cumulative counts.

- Explain arrival/departure effects.

- Identify repeat-entry or method checks.

## Practice 10 — standard · 3 marks

A fictional participant writes a memoir forty years after a strike. Give one potential value and two limitations for reconstructing decisions made during the strike.

Answer: It may reveal the participant's recollections and later interpretation. Memory can change over time, and later outcomes or self-presentation may shape the account.

1. Identify the author's access as a participant without assuming perfect recall.

2. Compare relevant claims with contemporaneous and independent evidence.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- State a question the memoir can illuminate.

- Explain a memory limitation.

- Explain hindsight or self-presentation and propose comparison.
