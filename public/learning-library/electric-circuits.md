## What travels and what is transferred

Current is the rate at which charge passes a cross-section: I=Q/t for constant current. One ampere is one coulomb per second. Conventional current direction follows positive-charge motion; electrons in a metal drift in the opposite direction. Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge, V=E/Q. Charge is not used up by a lamp or resistor. Instead, electrical energy is transferred to other forms while charge continues through the circuit. A complete conducting path is required for steady current, and the source maintains a potential difference rather than supplying a fixed current in every possible circuit.

## Series and parallel reasoning

In a series path the steady current is the same through each element because charge does not accumulate at a junction between them. Potential differences across elements add to the source potential difference in an ideal loop. Series resistances add. Across parallel branches, the potential difference is the same because each branch connects the same two nodes. Branch currents add to the total current. For resistors in parallel, 1/R_total is the sum of reciprocals; the equivalent resistance is smaller than the smallest branch resistance. Compare node connections rather than a drawing's visual shape when deciding which components are in series or parallel.

## Resistance, power and conditions

Resistance is R=V/I at a specified operating point. An ohmic resistor has a proportional current-voltage relationship when physical conditions, particularly temperature, remain constant. Not every component obeys a constant-resistance model: a filament lamp heats as current increases. Electrical power is P=VI. Combining this with V=IR gives P=I²R and P=V²/R for the same resistive operating condition. State what is held constant before comparing powers. Increasing resistance decreases power at fixed voltage, but increases power at fixed current. Energy transferred over a constant-power interval is E=Pt, with seconds needed for joules when power is in watts.

## Energy transfer, measurement and idealisation

Current is a rate of charge flow, whereas potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge between two points. A resistor transfers electrical energy to its surroundings but does not consume the charge flowing through it. In a steady series circuit, charge does not continually accumulate at an ordinary junction; the same current passes through successive components. At a junction in a steady network, total current entering equals total current leaving.

An ideal ammeter has negligible resistance and is connected in series with the branch being measured. An ideal voltmeter has very large resistance and is placed across a component, so it draws negligible extra current. Real meters depart from these idealisations and can change the circuit slightly. For an ohmic resistor under constant physical conditions, V=IR describes a straight-line relationship with constant resistance. A filament lamp changes temperature as current changes, so treating its resistance as constant over a wide range can be misleading. State whether a calculation assumes ideal wires, a source with negligible internal resistance and steady direct current.

## Checking series predictions with an energy balance

For ideal resistors in series, the same current passes through each component and their potential differences add to the supply potential difference. Resistance controls how the voltage is shared; identical current does not mean identical voltage or power. Use V = IR under the stated constant-resistance assumptions, then compare the sum of component powers with supply power. This provides a second check on arithmetic and the circuit model. Ideal connecting wires have no potential difference in this example. Real supplies can have internal resistance and components may heat up, so a disagreement in measured data needs investigation rather than an automatic claim that conservation has failed.

## Worked example 1

A 6 Ω and a 3 Ω resistor are in series across an ideal 18 V source. Find the current and voltage across the 6 Ω resistor.

1. Total resistance is 9 Ω.

2. Current is 18/9=2 A through both resistors.

3. The 6 Ω resistor has V=2×6=12 V.

Answer: 2 A and 12 V.

## Worked example 2

Two 6 Ω resistors are in parallel across 12 V. Find total current.

1. Each branch has 12 V across 6 Ω, so each carries 2 A.

2. Add branch currents: 2+2=4 A.

3. Check with equivalent resistance 3 Ω and I=12/3.

Answer: 4 A.

## Worked example 3

An ideal 12 V source supplies a 6 Ω resistor. Explain the current and energy transferred by 3 C of charge.

1. Use I=V/R to obtain 2 A.

2. Potential difference is energy per charge, so E=QV.

3. Calculate E=3×12=36 J, transferred from the source through the circuit.

Answer: Current 2 A; energy transferred for 3 C is 36 J.

## Worked example 4

Two ideal resistors, 4 Ω and 8 Ω, are in series across an ideal 6 V supply. Find the current, each voltage drop and total power.

1. Total resistance is 12 Ω, so current is 6/12 = 0.5 A.

2. Using V = IR gives 2 V across 4 Ω and 4 V across 8 Ω; the drops sum to 6 V.

3. Supply power is VI = 6 × 0.5 = 3 W, also equal to I²(4 + 8).

Answer: Current 0.5 A; voltage drops 2 V and 4 V; total power 3 W.

## Misconceptions

- Current is consumed as it passes through a resistor.
  Correction: Energy is transferred; steady charge flow is conserved.

- Adding a parallel branch increases total resistance.
  Correction: An additional conducting branch decreases equivalent resistance in the ideal resistor model.

## Flashcards

### What is electric current?
Charge passing per unit time, measured in amperes.

### What is potential difference?
Energy transferred per unit charge, measured in joules per coulomb or volts.

### Which quantity is equal through series components?
Steady current; their potential differences need not be equal.

### Which quantity is equal across parallel branches?
Potential difference between their common endpoint nodes.

### How does resistor power change if voltage doubles and resistance stays constant?
It quadruples because P=V²/R.

### Does a resistor use up electric charge?
No. It transfers energy; steady charge flow continues through the circuit.

### What is shared by components in a single series path?
The same current passes through every component.

### At equal current, how does resistor power depend on resistance?
P = I²R, so greater resistance gives greater power under the constant-resistance model.

## Practice 7 — extension · 3 marks

A learner says each series resistor must have 3 V because the current is the same. Explain the error and identify which resistor transfers more energy per second.

Answer: Equal current does not imply equal voltage. The 8 Ω resistor has 4 V and dissipates 2 W, compared with 1 W in the 4 Ω resistor.

1. At fixed current, V = IR makes the voltage drop proportional to resistance.

2. Power I²R is 0.25 × 8 = 2 W for the larger resistor.

3. The smaller resistor dissipates 1 W, consistent with total power 3 W.
