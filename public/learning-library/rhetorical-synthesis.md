# Selecting and combining notes for a rhetorical goal

## Prerequisites

- Identify a sentence's main claim and supporting details.

- Distinguish a comparison from a description or explanation.

## Objectives

- Translate a rhetorical goal into selection criteria.

- Combine relevant details without adding unsupported claims.

- Preserve comparisons, qualifiers and audience needs.

- Explain why a factually true option may still fail the task.

## The goal controls selection

Rhetorical synthesis means selecting and combining information to serve a specific communication purpose. Begin by identifying the task's action and focus: introduce a person, compare two results, illustrate a limitation or emphasise a shared feature. These are not interchangeable goals. A sentence that accurately reports a date may still be unsuitable when the goal is to compare methods. Treat the requested goal as a constraint on relevance, not as a vague invitation to include every interesting detail.

A useful mental template is: the response must tell this audience this relationship about these subjects. Fill those slots before evaluating candidate sentences. If the task asks for a similarity, an option describing only a difference misses the requested relationship. If it asks to introduce unfamiliar work, an unexplained pronoun or technical label may leave out information the audience needs.

## Relevance is not the same as truth

A note may be true within the supplied material but unnecessary for the requested sentence. Separate factual accuracy from rhetorical usefulness. Including all available notes often produces a crowded response whose main point is hard to find. Select the smallest set of details that establishes the goal and gives necessary context. A concise answer is effective when it preserves the important relationship, not merely because it has fewer words.

The reverse error is selecting a polished sentence that invents a stronger connection than the notes support. Two events occurring in sequence do not automatically prove that one caused the other. Two designs using the same material do not automatically have identical performance. Keep the task goal within the evidence rather than forcing the evidence to fit an attractive statement.

## Preserving comparisons and qualifiers

A fair comparison identifies what is being compared and keeps units, conditions and categories compatible. If one note reports output per hour and another reports output per day, do not compare the numbers without conversion. If a trial used a small sample, avoid replacing its result with a universal claim. Words such as some, under these conditions, estimated and approximately may carry essential information; deleting them can change the meaning.

Parallel sentence structure can clarify a comparison: one clause describes the first subject using a chosen criterion, and a matching clause describes the second. A contrast word is useful only when the details actually oppose each other on that criterion. When the goal is a shared feature, make both subjects explicit and state the common property rather than listing unrelated achievements.

## Audience and useful context

A reader unfamiliar with a person or project may need a brief identifying phrase before a result makes sense. A reader already familiar with it may instead benefit from a focused update. Supply context from the notes, not from assumed outside knowledge. Names, categories and technical terms should clarify the relationship, not merely make the response sound sophisticated.

Pronouns must have identifiable referents. A sentence beginning This was more efficient can be unclear if no device or comparison basis has been named. Replace vague reference with the specific subject and outcome. Where the task requests a general introduction, avoid burying the identity under a narrow statistic; where it requests a result, avoid spending the whole sentence on biography.

## A disciplined option-checking method

For each candidate, ask three separate questions: does it accomplish the stated goal, is every claim supported by the notes, and is the relationship expressed clearly? Rejecting an option for one reason does not establish that its facts are false. An irrelevant but accurate option is a different failure from an option that reverses two results. Naming the failure helps you improve your own revisions.

Do not reward complexity automatically. A long option may combine more notes while obscuring the requested point. Conversely, the shortest option may omit the comparison altogether. Check nouns and quantities carefully when two subjects appear in several notes: swapping which design produced which result creates a factual error even if every number appears somewhere in the material.

## Selecting notes for a comparison without adding causation

When a writing task asks for a comparison, select facts that describe both objects on the same dimension. A statement can be factually consistent with the notes yet fail the requested goal by discussing an unrelated detail. Preserve qualifiers such as in this sample or during the trial, since removing them broadens the claim. Do not turn a difference into a causal explanation unless the notes supply evidence for the cause. Draft the smallest sentence that accomplishes the stated purpose, then check each assertion against a particular note. The exercise uses original fictional notes and does not describe a real product trial.

## Preserve comparison units and the limits of the notes

A synthesis task asks you to select and connect supplied information for a stated purpose. Do not include every note automatically. Compare the same measure over the same interval, retain qualifiers such as in this trial, and avoid adding a causal explanation unless the notes support one. A sentence can be factually compatible with the notes yet miss the rhetorical goal by focusing on an irrelevant detail. After drafting, check each factual phrase against a specific note and check the whole sentence against the requested purpose.

## Worked example 1

Fictional notes: Mara designed a rain gauge; it uses recycled plastic; it was displayed in April. Goal: identify the designer and the material.

1. Select the designer and material notes.

2. The display date is true but not needed.

3. Combine without claiming an unreported performance benefit.

Answer: Mara designed a rain gauge made from recycled plastic.

## Worked example 2

Fictional notes: Model A weighs 2 kg and lasts 8 hours; Model B weighs 3 kg and lasts 12 hours. Goal: contrast operating time.

1. Identify duration as the comparison criterion.

2. Use 8 and 12 hours with the correct models.

3. Omit weight unless needed for the goal.

Answer: Model B operates for 12 hours, compared with Model A's 8 hours.

## Worked example 3

Fictional notes: both gardens used drip irrigation; only Garden N used shade cloth. Goal: state a shared method.

1. Find the property applying to both.

2. Do not turn the unique shade-cloth detail into a similarity.

3. Name both gardens or use an unambiguous collective subject.

Answer: Both gardens used drip irrigation.

## Worked example 4

Fictional notes: a pilot involved 18 volunteers; 12 preferred the new layout. Goal: report the pilot result faithfully.

1. Preserve the small-study boundary.

2. Report 12 of 18, not all users.

3. Avoid claiming the preference was caused by a particular design feature.

Answer: In the 18-volunteer pilot, 12 participants preferred the new layout.

## Worked example 5

Original notes: In a small trial, filter A removed 80% of the measured particles; filter B removed 92%; B weighed 2 kg; A was blue. Write one sentence comparing particle removal without inventing a cause.

1. Select the removal percentages because they share the requested comparison dimension.

2. Retain the small-trial qualifier.

3. Exclude weight and colour because they do not establish why removal differed.

Answer: In the small trial, filter B removed 92% of the measured particles, compared with 80% for filter A.

## Misconceptions

- The best synthesis includes all the notes.
  Correction: It includes the details needed for the particular goal.

- A factually true sentence must be the right answer.
  Correction: It may still fail the requested purpose or audience.

- A stronger claim is always more persuasive.
  Correction: Strength must not exceed what the notes support.

## Flashcards

### What should be identified before selecting notes?
The requested rhetorical goal, subject and audience.

### Why can an accurate option be unsuitable?
Its information may not accomplish the goal.

### What must a shared-feature sentence establish?
The same relevant property applies to both named subjects.

### What makes a comparison coherent?
A common criterion with compatible conditions and units.

### Why retain a qualifier such as in the pilot?
It limits the claim to the evidence actually supplied.

### When is a brief identification useful?
When the intended audience does not know the subject.

### Does a sequence of two events alone establish causation?
No.

### What is needed for a clear pronoun?
An identifiable, unambiguous referent.

### What three checks help evaluate a synthesis?
Goal fit, evidence support and clarity.

### How should the invented studies in this lesson be treated?
As fictional evidence packets, not real research citations.

### What makes two facts useful for a comparison?
They describe the compared objects on the same relevant dimension.

### Why preserve a trial qualifier in a synthesis?
It prevents a bounded observation from becoming an unsupported universal claim.

## Practice 1 — foundation · 1 marks

Fictional notes: Sensor P records every 2 minutes; Sensor Q records every 5 minutes; both use batteries. Goal: state a shared feature. Which sentence fits?

A. Sensor P records more frequently.

B. Sensor Q records every five minutes.

C. Both sensors use batteries.

D. Both sensors record every two minutes.

Answer: Both sensors use batteries.

1. Identify the requested similarity.

2. Only the power source is a shared feature in these notes.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Select the shared-feature sentence.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

Fictional notes: Jun built a folding bridge model from cardboard; it was photographed on Tuesday. Write one sentence identifying the creator and material.

Answer: Jun built a folding bridge model from cardboard.

2. Omit the date because it does not serve the goal.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Include Jun and the bridge model.

- Preserve cardboard as the material.

## Practice 4 — extension · 3 marks

A fictional survey of 25 club members found 16 preferred a later meeting. Explain why Everyone in the city wants later meetings is not a faithful synthesis.

Answer: The claim extends a 25-member club sample to the entire city and changes a 16-person preference into unanimity.

1. Identify the sampled group.

2. Compare the stated count with everyone.

3. Limit the conclusion to the supplied evidence.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify the population overreach.

- Identify the unanimity error.

- Give a suitably bounded interpretation.

## Practice 5 — standard · 2 marks

Fictional notes: both buildings have roof gardens; only Building R collects rainwater. Goal: emphasise a difference. Which detail should the sentence focus on?

2. Do not use the roof gardens as a contrast because both have them.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Select rainwater collection.

- Preserve the distinction.

## Practice 6 — extension · 3 marks

Fictional notes list a designer's birthplace, material choice and project cost. The goal asks only how the material differs from an earlier design. Explain an efficient selection strategy.

Answer: Choose the material information for both designs, identify their difference and omit birthplace or cost unless it is needed to understand that difference.

1. Translate the goal into a comparison criterion.

2. Select evidence for that criterion.

3. Remove unrelated information without calling it false.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify the material criterion.

- Require evidence for both sides.

- Explain omission of irrelevant facts.

## Practice 7 — standard · 2 marks

Fictional notes say output increased after a new manager arrived, but report no causal study. Why is The manager caused the increase stronger than the notes?

Answer: The notes establish sequence or association, not that the manager caused the change.

1. Identify what the chronology actually supports.

2. Distinguish it from a causal explanation.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- State the supported relationship.

- Reject unsupported causation.

## Practice 12 — standard · 3 marks

Fictional notes: Project Cedar restores a pond using volunteers; Project Birch restores a meadow using paid contractors; both aim to increase local habitat diversity. Goal: emphasise their common purpose. Write one sentence.

Answer: Both Project Cedar and Project Birch aim to increase local habitat diversity.

1. Identify the shared aim rather than the different habitats or staffing.

2. Retain aim to: the notes do not establish achieved outcomes.

- Name both projects.

- State the shared purpose.

- Preserve intended rather than demonstrated success.

## Practice 13 — extension · 3 marks

Fictional notes: A material resisted scratching in 8 of 10 laboratory trials; outdoor weathering was not tested. Goal: summarise the evidence and its limit. Write one sentence.

Answer: The material resisted scratching in 8 of 10 laboratory trials, but its outdoor weathering performance remains untested.

1. Include the tested outcome with its denominator and setting.

2. Name the untested condition without predicting its result.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Retain 8 of 10.

- Retain laboratory scratch testing.

- State outdoor weathering is untested.
