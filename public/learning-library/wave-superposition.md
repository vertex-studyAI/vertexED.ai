## What travels and what oscillates

A progressive wave transfers energy through a medium or field while a pattern propagates. In a simple wave on a string, each element oscillates about its equilibrium position; the element does not travel along the whole string with the pattern. Amplitude is the maximum magnitude of displacement, not the full peak-to-peak distance. Wavelength is a spatial repetition distance measured between neighbouring points in the same phase. Period is a temporal repetition interval at a fixed position.

In a transverse wave, displacement is perpendicular to the direction of propagation; in a longitudinal wave it is parallel. Sound in air is longitudinal, while an ideal wave on a stretched string is transverse. Electromagnetic waves involve oscillating fields and need no material medium. Polarisation restricts the direction of transverse oscillation and therefore supplies evidence for transverse behaviour. A longitudinal sound wave cannot be polarised in that same displacement-direction sense.

## Connecting speed, frequency and graphs

A travelling pattern moves one wavelength during one period, so v=λ/T=fλ and f=1/T. Convert units before substitution: a period of 4 ms is 0.004 s, corresponding to 250 Hz. Frequency is measured in cycles per second, whereas angular phase is expressed in degrees or radians. Do not confuse the speed of the propagating wave with the instantaneous speed of an oscillating particle.

A displacement-position graph at one instant can reveal wavelength but not period without extra information. A displacement-time graph at one position reveals period but not wavelength on its own. Always read the horizontal-axis label before measuring a separation. When a source frequency changes in a medium where the relevant wave speed is approximately fixed, the wavelength adjusts inversely. That is an assumption about the medium and regime, not a rule that every possible wave speed is frequency independent.

## Phase comparisons

For points along a sinusoidal progressive wave at the same instant, a separation Δx corresponds to phase magnitude 2πΔx/λ radians, or 360Δx/λ degrees. Phase differences differing by a whole number of cycles describe the same relative phase. A separation of one wavelength is therefore in phase; half a wavelength gives antiphase. Stating which point leads also requires the propagation direction and an agreed sign convention.

For observations at one position separated in time, use 2πΔt/T instead. Do not mix a time separation with wavelength. Phase is not the same as displacement: two points may have equal displacement while moving in opposite directions and therefore not be in phase. For a complete phase comparison, the state of oscillation includes both displacement and direction of motion.

## Superposition creates stationary patterns

Within the linear approximation, overlapping wave displacements add algebraically. Two identical-frequency waves of equal amplitude travelling in opposite directions can produce a stationary pattern. At nodes the displacements cancel at every time; at antinodes the oscillation amplitude is largest. For an ideal equal-amplitude pair, an antinode reaches twice the amplitude of either contributing wave. That does not imply every point always has that displacement.

Adjacent nodes are separated by λ/2, and a node to the nearest antinode by λ/4, where λ is the wavelength of the contributing travelling waves. The pattern's nodes stay in place even though the constituent waves propagate. Points within one node-to-node loop oscillate in phase, while neighbouring loops oscillate in antiphase. The ideal stationary pattern carries no net time-averaged energy along the string; real driven systems still require input to offset dissipative losses.

## Harmonics of a fixed-end string

If both ends of an ideal string are nodes, the length L contains an integer number n of half-wavelengths: L=nλ/2. Thus λn=2L/n and fn=nv/(2L). For tension F and mass per unit length μ, the ideal string speed is √(F/μ), giving f1=(1/(2L))√(F/μ). Use first harmonic for n=1 in the AQA terminology mapped here. The symbol for tension is sometimes T, so distinguish it from period when reading a formula.

Holding other variables fixed, doubling length halves the first-harmonic frequency, quadrupling tension doubles it, and quadrupling linear density halves it. These predictions depend on the ideal-string assumptions. In a supervised investigation, repeated measurements and a graph can test the predicted relationship; changing several factors at once obscures which caused a shift. ## Distinguishing a harmonic number from adjacent nodes

A stationary wave on an ideal string fixed at both ends has nodes at the ends. Adjacent nodes are separated by half a wavelength, so the nth harmonic fits n half-wavelengths into the string length. Count intervals rather than just internal nodes. Once wavelength is found, use wave speed divided by wavelength for frequency. Increasing harmonic number at fixed length and speed increases frequency, while the distance between adjacent nodes decreases. The stationary pattern has fixed nodes and antinodes; it does not mean the string is motionless everywhere. The example assumes an ideal uniform string and fixed wave speed, rather than modelling tension changes or damping.

## Add signed displacements and identify the boundary conditions

For linear superposition, instantaneous displacements add algebraically. Opposite signs may cancel at one place and time without making either travelling wave disappear. A stationary pattern has persistent nodes because interference repeatedly cancels there. Allowed wavelengths depend on the boundary conditions: a string fixed at both ends differs from an ideal air column closed at one end. State the system before using a harmonic formula. Frequency, phase and displacement are distinct quantities, so do not infer a wave's complete behaviour from one instantaneous measurement.

## Worked example 1

A wave has f=80 Hz and λ=0.45 m. Find its speed.

1. Use v=fλ.

2. Multiply 80 by 0.45.

3. Frequency times wavelength has units m/s.

Answer: 36 m/s.

## Worked example 2

Two points on a progressive wave are separated by 0.15 m, with wavelength 0.60 m. Find phase magnitude.

1. The separation is one quarter of a wavelength.

2. Use 2π×0.15/0.60.

3. No lead/lag sign is inferred without a direction convention.

Answer: π/2 radians, or 90°.

## Worked example 3

A 0.90 m string fixed at both ends shows three loops. Wave speed is 120 m/s. Find frequency.

1. Three loops give n=3 and λ=2L/n=0.60 m.

2. Use f=v/λ.

3. Check this is the third harmonic.

Answer: 200 Hz.

## Worked example 4

Tension is 64 N, linear density is 0.004 kg/m and length is 1 m. Find first-harmonic frequency.

1. v=√(64/0.004)=√16000 m/s.

2. f1=v/(2×1).

3. Round only after evaluating the square root.

Answer: About 63.25 Hz.

## Worked example 5

A 1.5 m ideal string fixed at both ends vibrates in its third harmonic with wave speed 90 m s⁻¹. Find wavelength, frequency and adjacent-node spacing.

1. Three half-wavelengths fill 1.5 m, so λ = 2L/3 = 1.0 m.

2. Frequency is v/λ = 90/1.0 = 90 Hz.

3. Adjacent nodes are λ/2 = 0.5 m apart.

Answer: Wavelength 1.0 m; frequency 90 Hz; adjacent-node spacing 0.5 m.

## Misconceptions

- A node means no waves pass through that location.
  Correction: The contributing waves overlap there and their displacements cancel.

- The distance between adjacent nodes is one wavelength.
  Correction: It is half the wavelength of either contributing travelling wave.

- Frequency and wave speed are the same quantity.
  Correction: Frequency counts cycles per time; speed measures propagation distance per time.

## Flashcards

### How does amplitude compare with peak-to-peak displacement?
Amplitude is half the peak-to-peak distance for a symmetric oscillation.

### How are frequency and period related?
f=1/T.

### What connects wave speed, frequency and wavelength?
v=fλ.

### What phase magnitude corresponds to λ/4 separation?
π/2 radians or 90°.

### What does polarisation reveal about a wave?
It has transverse oscillations whose directions can be restricted.

### What is a node in an ideal stationary pattern?
A point of zero oscillation amplitude due to persistent cancellation.

### How far apart are adjacent nodes?
λ/2.

### What amplitude occurs at an antinode for two equal-amplitude component waves?
Twice either component amplitude.

### What is fn for a fixed-end string with speed v and length L?
nv/(2L), for positive integer n.

### How does first-harmonic frequency change when tension is quadrupled?
It doubles if length and linear density stay fixed.

### What does harmonic number count on a fixed-end ideal string?
The number of half-wavelength intervals along the string.

### Is every point motionless in a stationary wave?
No. Nodes remain at zero displacement while other points oscillate with position-dependent amplitudes.
