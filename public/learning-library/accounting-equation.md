## The business as a separate accounting entity

Accounting records the business's transactions separately from the owner's personal transactions, even when the owner is legally responsible for the business. Assets are economic resources controlled by the business as a result of past events. Liabilities are present obligations to transfer economic resources. Equity is the residual interest after liabilities are deducted from assets. The accounting equation is assets = liabilities + equity. It is a statement of relationships, not a claim that all assets are cash or that an asset could necessarily be sold for its recorded amount. An owner's contribution increases business assets and equity; it is not sales revenue.

## Two effects and the debit-credit convention

Every recorded transaction preserves the accounting equation. Buying equipment for cash exchanges one asset for another: equipment increases while cash decreases. Buying equipment on credit increases an asset and a liability. Paying a supplier reduces cash and the payable together. Double entry records equal total debits and credits, but debit does not mean bad and credit does not mean good. Asset and expense accounts normally increase with debits; liability, equity and revenue accounts normally increase with credits. Their decreases use the opposite side. Start by identifying the accounts and whether each increases or decreases before assigning debit or credit. Equal totals alone do not prove that the correct accounts or amounts were used.

## Performance is not the cash balance

Profit is revenue less expenses for the accounting period under the applicable recognition rules. Cash can rise because the owner contributes funds or the business borrows, neither of which is revenue. Cash can fall when equipment is purchased or a loan is repaid without the entire payment being a current expense. In a simple sole-trader model, closing equity equals opening equity plus capital introduced plus profit minus drawings, assuming no other equity changes. Drawings are resources withdrawn by the owner for personal use, not a business operating expense. Distinguish revenue earned from customer cash collected: a credit sale may create a receivable before the customer pays. These distinctions explain why a statement of financial position and an income statement answer different questions.

## Errors, evidence and the limits of balancing

A balanced accounting equation or trial balance is a useful arithmetic check, but it does not establish complete or accurate records. A transaction omitted entirely changes neither side of a trial balance. An expense posted to the wrong expense account can preserve equality. Recording the same incorrect amount on both sides can also leave totals balanced. Verification therefore requires evidence about what happened, which accounts were affected and when the transaction belongs in the records.

Source documents such as invoices and receipts support entries, but their meaning must be interpreted. An invoice may establish an obligation or a credit sale; a later payment settles a balance rather than necessarily creating a new expense or revenue item. Reconcile movements in cash and balances with supporting records, and distinguish a correction from a new transaction. Keep the business-entity assumption visible when reviewing owner-related payments. A payment for the owner's personal use should not be treated as an operating cost merely because it passed through the business bank account. This lesson uses simplified examples and does not substitute for jurisdiction-specific accounting standards.

## Credit purchases, cash payments and expense recognition

A transaction can change the composition of assets without changing their total. Buying equipment for cash exchanges one asset for another; it is not automatically an expense of the whole purchase price on that date. Buying equipment on credit increases both assets and liabilities. Paying that supplier later reduces cash and the liability, without creating a second equipment purchase. Separate these events by date and identify the account affected before deciding which side is debited. The accounting equation is a consistency check, not proof that the correct accounts were used: a balanced entry can still misclassify an asset as an expense. Depreciation and tax treatment are outside this example.

## Worked example 1

An owner contributes 5000 cash and the business then borrows 2000. Show the accounting equation.

1. The contribution creates cash assets of 5000 and equity of 5000.

2. Borrowing adds 2000 cash and a 2000 liability.

3. Assets are now 7000 = liabilities 2000 + equity 5000.

Answer: 7000 = 2000 + 5000; neither receipt is sales revenue.

## Worked example 2

The business buys equipment costing 1200 for cash. Record the two account effects.

1. Equipment, an asset, increases by 1200: debit equipment.

2. Cash, another asset, decreases by 1200: credit cash.

3. Total assets and the accounting equation are unchanged at the transaction date.

Answer: Debit equipment 1200; credit cash 1200.

## Worked example 3

An owner withdraws 200 cash for personal use. Explain the effect in the simplified sole-trader model.

1. Cash, an asset, decreases by 200.

2. Drawings reduce equity rather than operating profit.

3. The accounting equation remains balanced with assets and equity both lower.

Answer: Assets and equity fall by 200; the withdrawal is not an operating expense.

## Worked example 4

A business starts with cash of 9,000 and capital of 9,000. It buys equipment for 2,400 on credit, then pays the supplier 900. State final cash, equipment, liabilities and capital, and check the accounting equation.

1. The credit purchase adds equipment 2,400 and a payable 2,400.

2. The payment reduces cash to 8,100 and the payable to 1,500.

3. Total assets are 10,500, equal to liabilities plus unchanged capital.

Answer: Cash 8,100; equipment 2,400; liabilities 1,500; capital 9,000. Assets 10,500 = 1,500 + 9,000.

## Misconceptions

- Every cash receipt is revenue.
  Correction: Borrowing and owner contributions increase cash without being sales revenue.

- A balanced trial balance proves there are no errors.
  Correction: Omissions, incorrect accounts and equal errors on both sides can leave totals balanced.

## Flashcards

### State the accounting equation.
Assets = liabilities + equity.

### What happens when equipment is bought on credit?
Equipment assets and liabilities both increase by the transaction amount.

### Why are owner drawings not an operating expense?
They distribute resources to the owner rather than measure resources consumed to earn business revenue.

### Which side normally records an increase in an asset?
A debit; a decrease in an asset is normally a credit.

### Why is the cash balance not a measure of profit?
Cash movements include financing and asset exchanges, while revenue and expense recognition can occur before or after cash settlement.

### What does agreement of trial-balance totals establish?
Only that recorded debit and credit totals agree; omissions and several types of misclassification can remain.

### Does settling a supplier payable create another purchase?
No. It reduces cash and the existing liability.

### Why can a balanced entry still be wrong?
Equal debits and credits do not prove that the chosen accounts or recognition date are correct.
