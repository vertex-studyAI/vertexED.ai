## A selective, dynamic boundary

Phospholipids form a bilayer with hydrophilic heads towards aqueous surroundings and hydrophobic tails facing inward. Small non-polar molecules can cross the hydrophobic interior more easily than charged ions. Proteins form channels, carriers and receptors; glycoproteins and glycolipids contribute to recognition. Components can move laterally, making the membrane fluid rather than a rigid tiled wall. Cholesterol restricts molecular movement and influences permeability; it is not itself a pore for glucose.

## Passive and active routes

Simple diffusion proceeds down a concentration gradient through the bilayer. Facilitated diffusion also proceeds down a gradient but uses specific channels or carriers; neither directly requires ATP hydrolysis. A carrier can become saturated when all its binding sites are occupied. Active transport moves a substance against its concentration gradient using carrier proteins and energy from ATP hydrolysis. Osmosis is net water movement across a partially permeable membrane from higher to lower water potential. A more negative potential is lower, so water moves from -200 kPa towards -500 kPa when other conditions permit.

## Co-transport in the ileum

The sodium-potassium pump in the membrane facing the blood uses ATP to export sodium from an epithelial cell. This maintains a lower sodium concentration inside than in the intestinal lumen. Sodium can then enter down its gradient through a co-transporter that also brings glucose into the cell, potentially against the glucose gradient. Glucose can leave towards the blood by facilitated diffusion. The co-transporter is indirectly dependent on ATP: do not claim that it necessarily hydrolyses ATP itself.

## Practical inference and its limits

In a plant-tissue investigation, use equal initial dimensions, controlled immersion time and temperature, blot consistently and calculate percentage mass change. The concentration giving zero mean change estimates the tissue’s initial water potential when calibrated against solutions of known potential. Replicates and uncertainty matter because a zero crossing between widely separated points is imprecise.

For a beetroot permeability experiment, wash cut surfaces consistently before treatment, control pigment-containing tissue size and exposure time, and compare colour using a calibrated colorimeter. Increasing temperature may disrupt membrane organisation and denature proteins, releasing pigment. Colour intensity measures leaked pigment under controlled conditions, not membrane thickness or the exact number of broken proteins.

## Worked example 1

A tissue sample begins at 2.50 g and ends at 2.20 g. Interpret the percentage change.

1. Change = -0.30 g; divide by original 2.50 g.

2. Multiply by 100 and explain direction, without treating one sample as proof of a precise water potential.

Answer: -12%; net water loss is consistent with a lower external water potential.

## Worked example 2

An ATP inhibitor reduces glucose absorption from the ileum. Explain the indirect connection.

1. Locate ATP use at the sodium-potassium pump.

2. Link the reduced sodium gradient to reduced sodium-glucose entry.

Answer: The sodium gradient declines because the pump cannot maintain it, reducing co-transport.

## Misconceptions

- Facilitated diffusion always uses ATP.
  Correction: It is passive movement down a gradient through a protein.

- Co-transport means glucose diffuses through the sodium-potassium pump.
  Correction: The pump establishes a gradient; a different protein couples sodium and glucose entry.

## Flashcards

### Why do ions need proteins to cross readily?
Their charge is incompatible with the hydrophobic bilayer interior.

### What does ATP power in ileal uptake?
The pump maintaining the sodium gradient.

### Which way does water move?
From higher to lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane.

### Why can carrier transport saturate?
A finite number of carriers has a finite cycling rate.

### What does beetroot absorbance indicate?
Pigment leakage under the stated controlled conditions.

## Practice 2 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] A cell has water potential -350 kPa and its surrounding solution -100 kPa. Predict net water movement.

Answer: Into the cell.

1. -100 is higher than -350.

2. Water moves down the water-potential gradient across the membrane.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Correct ordering.

- Movement inward.

## Practice 3 — extension · 3 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Predict how inhibiting the sodium-potassium pump affects sodium-glucose co-transport after existing gradients diminish.

Answer: Glucose uptake decreases as the inward sodium gradient weakens.

1. ATP-dependent export normally keeps intracellular sodium low.

2. Less downhill sodium movement supplies less coupled transport.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Pump role.

- Gradient weakens.

- Reduced glucose uptake.

## Practice 4 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] A potato cylinder changes from 4.00 g to 4.28 g. Calculate percentage change and state a consistent water-potential explanation.

Answer: +7.0%; surrounding solution initially had higher water potential.

1. 0.28 / 4.00 × 100 = 7.0.

2. Net entry of water explains increased mass.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- +7.0%.

- Higher external potential.

## Practice 5 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] One beetroot sample is twice as large as another in equal water volumes. Why can their absorbances not isolate temperature’s effect?

Answer: Different tissue amounts can release different pigment quantities independent of temperature.

1. Identify tissue amount as an uncontrolled variable.

2. Standardise tissue size and other conditions before attributing differences to temperature.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Tissue-amount confound.

- Suitable control.

## Practice 6 — extension · 3 marks

[A-Level: untiered] A transport-rate graph plateaus as external solute concentration rises. Explain a carrier-based mechanism and why the plateau alone does not prove active transport.

Answer: Carriers may be saturated; facilitated diffusion can also saturate.

1. All binding sites can be occupied at a finite turnover rate.

2. Energy dependence and gradient direction require separate evidence.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Finite carrier capacity.

- Passive carrier route also possible.

- Additional evidence identified.
