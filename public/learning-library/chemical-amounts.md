## Amount of substance

A mole is a unit for amount of substance. It counts a specified kind of entity, which might be atoms, molecules, ions or formula units. State the entity when interpreting a count. The relationship n=m/M converts mass m to amount n using molar mass M. If mass is in grams, use molar mass in grams per mole. A larger molar mass means fewer moles for the same mass; it does not mean a mole contains more entities. Chemical formulas establish the ratios of elements within the entity, so one mole of water molecules contains two moles of hydrogen atoms and one mole of oxygen atoms.

## Equations encode particle ratios

A balanced chemical equation conserves the number of atoms of each element. Change coefficients in front of formulas to balance it; changing a subscript changes the chemical substance. Coefficients give ratios of particles and therefore ratios of moles. In 2H₂+O₂→2H₂O, two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to form two moles of water, assuming complete reaction by this equation. The coefficients are not directly mass ratios because the substances have different molar masses. A reliable calculation converts the known mass into moles, applies the coefficient ratio, and then converts product moles into the requested mass.

## Limiting quantity and yield

When amounts of more than one reactant are given, determine which runs out first. Divide each available amount in moles by its reaction coefficient; the smallest value limits the extent of reaction. Excess reactant remains after the limiting reactant has been consumed. The theoretical yield assumes the stated reaction proceeds completely with no product loss or competing reaction. Actual isolated mass may be lower through incomplete reaction, side reactions or transfer losses. Percentage yield is actual yield divided by theoretical yield times 100, using comparable units and the same product. A value above 100% suggests issues such as wet or impure product or an incorrect calculation, not creation of extra atoms.

## Yield, purity and the limiting-reactant workflow

A balanced equation links amounts in moles, not masses in grams. Convert each reactant to moles before comparing its available amount with its stoichiometric coefficient. The smallest available-moles-to-coefficient ratio determines the limiting reaction extent. Use that extent to calculate the theoretical product amount, then convert to mass if required. Another reactant may remain in excess; calling it excess does not mean it was chemically irrelevant.

Percentage yield compares actual isolated product with theoretical product, using the same units and chemical identity in both quantities. A low yield can reflect incomplete reaction, competing processes or loss during separation; it does not by itself identify which occurred. A reported yield above 100% usually signals issues such as contamination, retained solvent, an incorrect product assumption or measurement error, rather than production of extra matter. If a sample is impure, only the reactive fraction contributes to its available reactant amount under the stated assumptions. Keep purity, yield and atom economy distinct: they answer different questions about starting material, recovered product and the reaction's allocation of atoms.

## Finding an excess reactant after a reaction

The limiting reactant determines the maximum extent of reaction. Divide each available amount by its stoichiometric coefficient and compare the resulting possible reaction extents. Use the smaller extent to calculate product and to find how much of the other reactant was consumed. Subtracting mole amounts directly is valid only when the equation demands a one-to-one ratio. Keep mass conversion separate from the ratio stage: first convert each mass to amount, then apply the balanced equation, and finally convert an amount to mass if needed. A theoretical yield assumes the modelled reaction reaches the stated extent without product loss or competing reactions. It is not a measured yield.

## Worked example 1

Find the amount in 9.0 g of water using M(H₂O)=18.0 g/mol.

1. Use n=m/M.

2. Divide 9.0 g by 18.0 g/mol.

3. Check that grams cancel, leaving moles.

Answer: 0.50 mol H₂O.

## Worked example 2

For 2H₂+O₂→2H₂O, 3 mol H₂ and 2 mol O₂ are available. Identify the limiting reactant and product amount.

1. Compare 3/2=1.5 with 2/1=2 reaction extents.

2. Hydrogen gives the smaller extent and is limiting.

3. Water amount is 2×1.5=3 mol; oxygen consumed is 1.5 mol.

Answer: Hydrogen is limiting; 3 mol water form and 0.5 mol oxygen remain.

## Worked example 3

A calculation predicts 10 g of dry product, but 8 g is isolated. Find percentage yield and state a limitation of the result.

1. Use actual mass divided by theoretical mass, multiplied by 100.

2. Calculate 8/10×100=80%.

3. The result quantifies recovery relative to the model but does not identify the cause of the loss.

Answer: 80% yield; the mechanism of the shortfall remains undetermined.

## Worked example 4

For 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, start with 5 mol H2 and 2 mol O2. Identify the limiting reactant, the theoretical water amount and the hydrogen remaining.

1. Possible extents are 5/2 = 2.5 from hydrogen and 2/1 = 2 from oxygen.

2. At extent 2, the reaction forms 2 × 2 = 4 mol water and consumes 4 mol hydrogen.

3. The remaining hydrogen is 5 - 4 = 1 mol.

Answer: O2 is limiting; 4 mol H2O forms; 1 mol H2 remains.

## Misconceptions

- Reaction coefficients give mass ratios.
  Correction: They give particle or mole ratios; molar masses are needed for mass comparisons.

- The smaller reactant mass is always limiting.
  Correction: Compare available moles relative to the stoichiometric coefficients.

## Practice 7 — extension · 3 marks

A learner subtracts 2 mol oxygen from 5 mol hydrogen and reports 3 mol hydrogen remaining. Explain and correct the ratio error.

Answer: Each mole of oxygen consumes two moles of hydrogen, so 2 mol oxygen consumes 4 mol hydrogen and leaves 1 mol.

1. The balanced coefficients specify a 2:1 hydrogen-to-oxygen amount ratio.

2. Multiply the oxygen amount by 2 to find hydrogen consumed.

3. Subtract consumed hydrogen from its own initial amount, not from the oxygen amount.
