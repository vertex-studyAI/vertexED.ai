## Random motion and net movement

Particles move randomly because of their thermal motion. If more particles are present on one side of a region, random movement initially produces more crossings away from that side than toward it. This creates net diffusion down a concentration gradient even though individual particles move in both directions. At dynamic equilibrium, motion continues but the opposing rates are equal, so there is no net change. Diffusion is faster when the gradient is steeper, the available area is larger or the diffusion distance is shorter, other conditions being equal. Higher temperature often increases particle motion, but biological systems may also change when heated.

## Membranes and osmosis

A cell membrane is selectively permeable: different substances cross at different rates depending on properties such as size, charge and available transport proteins. Osmosis is net water movement through a selectively permeable membrane. For simple solutions at the same pressure with nonpenetrating solutes, water tends to move from the more dilute solution toward the more concentrated solution. More advanced descriptions use water potential because pressure also matters. A plant cell placed in sufficiently dilute surroundings can become turgid as its contents press against the cell wall. An animal cell lacks that rigid wall and may swell excessively. State the solution conditions instead of memorising a direction without context.

## Transport proteins and experimental evidence

Facilitated diffusion uses membrane proteins but remains passive: net transport follows the relevant concentration or electrochemical gradient. Active transport can move substances against that gradient and requires an energy source, directly or through another coupled gradient. The presence of a protein alone therefore does not distinguish active from passive transport. To investigate water movement in tissue, compare a controlled measure such as percentage mass change, calculated relative to initial mass. Keep sample dimensions, time and temperature comparable and use repeats. A mass change supports an inference about net exchange, but does not directly show individual molecules moving or establish that every observed effect was caused by osmosis alone.

## Interpreting experiments without confusing mechanisms

A change in tissue mass is an observation, not a direct measurement of every molecule crossing its membranes. In a simplified osmosis experiment, water movement can explain much of the mass change, but damaged cells, evaporation, surface liquid and dissolved substances leaving the tissue can also affect measurements. Standardise the time, temperature, starting dimensions and blotting procedure when comparing samples. Percentage mass change helps compare different starting masses, but it does not remove all differences between samples.

The concentration producing approximately zero net mass change can estimate an isotonic condition for the particular tissue and experimental conditions. It does not imply that water molecules stopped moving or that every cell has an identical internal composition. A concentration gradient can change as transport occurs, reducing the initial rate. Active transport differs because cells use energy to move selected substances against their electrochemical gradients through membrane proteins. Do not infer active transport solely from the presence of a membrane: passive diffusion, facilitated diffusion and osmosis also involve membranes. Match the proposed mechanism to the substance, direction of movement and evidence about energy dependence.

## Using relative mass change in an osmosis comparison

Compare percentage mass changes when tissue pieces begin with different masses. The denominator is the initial mass, because the question is how large the change is relative to the starting amount. A negative result indicates net mass loss, not a negative amount of tissue. In an osmosis investigation, standardise blotting before weighing so surface liquid does not masquerade as tissue water uptake. A near-zero change is consistent with little net water movement under those conditions; water molecules can still cross in both directions. Replicates, comparable tissue pieces and controlled exposure times make the comparison more interpretable. These are fictional data, not evidence that an actual investigation was performed.

## Worked example 1

A tissue sample increases from 2.0 g to 2.3 g. Calculate its percentage mass change.

1. Mass change is final minus initial: 0.3 g.

2. Divide by initial mass and multiply by 100: 0.3/2.0×100.

3. Retain the positive sign to show gain.

Answer: +15%.

## Worked example 2

Two equal-pressure solutions are separated by a membrane permeable to water but not their dissolved solute. Side A is more dilute. Predict initial net water movement.

1. The membrane permits water exchange but prevents solute equilibration by crossing.

2. Under the stated simple conditions, net water movement is from the more dilute to the more concentrated solution.

3. Individual water molecules still move both ways.

Answer: Initially net movement is from A toward the more concentrated side.

## Worked example 3

A tissue sample has no net mass change after immersion. Explain why this does not show that its membranes are impermeable to water.

1. Net change measures the balance of movements, not the absence of movement.

2. Water can cross in both directions at equal average rates.

3. Other controls are needed to distinguish dynamic balance from a barrier that prevents movement.

Answer: Zero net mass change is compatible with continuing water exchange.

## Worked example 4

In fictional tissue data, piece A changes from 2.00 g to 2.20 g and piece B from 4.00 g to 4.24 g. Calculate both percentage changes and identify the larger relative gain.

1. A changes by 0.20 g, so its percentage change is 0.20/2.00 × 100 = 10%.

2. B changes by 0.24 g, so its percentage change is 0.24/4.00 × 100 = 6%.

3. Compare percentages rather than the unequal starting masses or absolute changes.

Answer: A gains 10%; B gains 6%. A has the larger relative gain.

## Misconceptions

- Particles stop moving at equilibrium.
  Correction: Movement continues, but opposing rates balance.

- Every process using a membrane protein needs metabolic energy.
  Correction: Facilitated diffusion uses proteins without moving against the relevant gradient.

## Flashcards

### Why say net movement in diffusion?
Individual particles cross both ways; the difference between opposing rates creates the net flow.

### What additional condition distinguishes osmosis from ordinary diffusion in solution?
Water moves through a selectively permeable membrane.

### What distinguishes active transport from facilitated diffusion?
Active transport can move against a relevant gradient using an energy source.

### Why can a plant cell become turgid without immediately bursting?
The cell wall resists expansion as pressure develops.

### Which mass is the denominator in percentage mass change?
Initial mass, not final mass.

### Does zero net mass change mean no water crossed a membrane?
No. It can reflect equal average movement in opposite directions.

### Which mass is the denominator for percentage mass change?
The initial mass: (final - initial)/initial × 100%.

### Does zero net water movement mean molecular motion stops?
No. Movements in opposite directions can balance.
