## Amounts are counts, not masses

A mole contains the Avogadro number of specified entities. Name those entities: one mole of oxygen molecules contains two moles of oxygen atoms. Amount n = m/M uses mass in grams with molar mass in grams per mole. Relative formula mass itself has no unit; molar mass does. The same mass of two substances generally contains different amounts because their molar masses differ. Retain extra digits during working and round the final result to a precision justified by the data.

## Solutions and reaction ratios

For concentration c in mol dm^-3, n = cV requires V in dm^3. Divide a volume in cm^3 by 1000. A balanced equation supplies mole ratios, not mass ratios. In H2SO4 + 2NaOH → Na2SO4 + 2H2O, one mole of acid reacts with two of sodium hydroxide. First find a measured reagent’s amount, apply the stoichiometric ratio, and only then calculate the unknown concentration. A limiting reactant is the one supplying the smallest amount divided by its equation coefficient.

## Gas and formula reasoning

In pV = nRT, use pressure in pascals, volume in cubic metres and temperature in kelvin with R = 8.314 J mol^-1 K^-1. A room-temperature molar-volume shortcut is not valid for arbitrary pressure and temperature. Real gases depart from ideal behaviour, especially where intermolecular attractions or particle volume matter.

An empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number atom ratio. Convert each element’s mass to amount before dividing by the smallest amount. Ratios such as 1:1.5 need multiplication by two, not careless rounding. Divide molecular mass by empirical-formula mass to obtain the integer multiplier for a molecular formula.

## Titration quality

Prepare a standard solution by quantitative transfer into a volumetric flask, rinse transferred material into it, dissolve fully and make up to the mark with the meniscus read at eye level. Mix thoroughly. Rinse the pipette with the solution it will deliver and the burette with its titrant; residual water there dilutes measured solutions. Distilled water added to the conical flask changes volume but not the amount originally pipetted. Use concordant titres, excluding the rough titre; do not conceal a poor spread by averaging everything. A titre is the difference between two readings, so both contribute to uncertainty.

## Worked example 1

25.0 cm^3 of H2SO4 needs 20.0 cm^3 of 0.100 mol dm^-3 NaOH. Find acid concentration.

1. NaOH amount = 0.100 × 0.0200 = 0.00200 mol.

2. Acid amount = 0.00100 mol; divide by 0.0250 dm^3.

Answer: 0.0400 mol dm^-3.

## Worked example 2

A compound is 40.0% C, 6.67% H, 53.33% O by mass. Use Ar C=12, H=1, O=16.

1. On a 100 g basis, amounts are about 3.333, 6.67 and 3.333 mol.

2. Divide by 3.333 to obtain approximately 1:2:1.

Answer: Empirical formula CH2O.

## Misconceptions

- Equal solution volumes contain equal moles.
  Correction: Concentration also matters: n = cV.

- Adding water to the conical flask changes the titre needed.
  Correction: It does not change the amount pipetted, provided no analyte is lost and the same endpoint chemistry applies.

## Practice 1 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Find the amount in 5.85 g NaCl using molar mass 58.5 g mol^-1.

Answer: 0.100 mol.

1. n = m/M.

2. 5.85 / 58.5 = 0.100.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Correct division.

- 0.100 mol.

## Practice 2 — extension · 3 marks

[A-Level: untiered] 25.0 cm^3 sulfuric acid reacts with 30.0 cm^3 of 0.200 mol dm^-3 NaOH. Use H2SO4 + 2NaOH → Na2SO4 + 2H2O to find acid concentration.

Answer: 0.120 mol dm^-3.

1. NaOH amount = 0.00600 mol; acid amount = 0.00300 mol.

2. Divide by 0.0250 dm^3.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Volume converted.

- 2:1 ratio applied.

- 0.120 mol dm^-3.

## Practice 3 — extension · 3 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Calculate the volume of 0.500 mol ideal gas at 300 K and 100000 Pa, R=8.314 J mol^-1 K^-1.

Answer: 0.0125 m^3, to three significant figures.

1. V = nRT/p.

2. 0.500 × 8.314 × 300 / 100000 = 0.012471 m^3.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Rearrangement.

- Correct substitution.

- 0.0125 m^3.

## Practice 4 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] A compound has empirical formula CH2O and molecular mass 180. Use Ar C=12, H=1, O=16. Find its molecular formula.

Answer: C6H12O6.

1. Empirical-formula mass = 30.

2. 180 / 30 = 6; multiply all subscripts by six.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Multiplier 6.

- C6H12O6.

## Practice 5 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Each burette reading has uncertainty ±0.05 cm^3. Estimate the maximum percentage uncertainty in a 20.00 cm^3 titre.

Answer: 0.50%.

1. Two readings give a maximum uncertainty of ±0.10 cm^3.

2. 0.10 / 20.00 × 100 = 0.50%.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Two uncertainties included.

- 0.50%.

## Practice 6 — extension · 2 marks

[A-Level: untiered] Explain why rinsing a conical flask with distilled water is acceptable but leaving water in a burette before adding titrant is problematic.

Answer: Flask water preserves analyte amount; burette water reduces titrant concentration.

1. The measured analyte is already present in a fixed amount.

2. Dilution in the burette changes moles delivered per measured volume.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Amount preserved in flask.

- Titrant concentration changed.
