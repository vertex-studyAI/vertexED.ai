## Attraction and structure

Bonding models explain how electrostatic attractions can stabilise a collection of particles. An ionic solid consists of oppositely charged ions in an extended lattice; it is not a collection of isolated molecule-sized ion pairs. A covalent bond involves shared electron density attracted to nuclei. Covalent substances can consist of separate molecules or extended networks, so the word covalent alone does not predict a low melting point. A metallic model describes positive ion cores with delocalised electrons. Each description is a simplified model useful for explaining selected properties, not a literal photograph of static particles.

## Charge carriers and conductivity

Electrical conduction requires charged particles able to move through the material. In a solid ionic lattice, ions are constrained around lattice positions and cannot carry a sustained current through the bulk. When the ionic substance melts, ions become mobile and can conduct. Dissolution can also produce mobile ions when the substance dissolves and dissociates. Metals have mobile delocalised electrons in the solid state. Many molecular substances lack mobile charges and conduct poorly. Graphite is an important counterexample to the claim that all covalent substances are insulators: its bonding permits mobile electrons within layers. State both the charge carrier and its mobility rather than merely naming a bond type.

## Melting and mechanical properties

Melting a simple molecular substance primarily overcomes attractions between molecules rather than breaking all covalent bonds within each molecule. The size and nature of intermolecular attractions help explain differences in boiling and melting behaviour. Melting an ionic solid requires substantial disruption of strong attractions throughout its lattice, although numerical melting points depend on the particular substance. Diamond is a covalent network with strong bonds extending through three dimensions, explaining its high hardness. Ionic crystals can fracture when a displaced layer brings like charges next to each other, creating repulsion. A good explanation links a specified structure to a mechanism and then to the observed property; a label such as strong bonds by itself is incomplete.

## Explaining exceptions and comparing evidence

A structure-property explanation should compare specific substances under stated conditions. Saying that one substance has stronger bonds can be inadequate if the process being discussed does not break those bonds. For example, differences between molecular boiling points usually require discussion of intermolecular attractions, molecular shape and opportunities for close contact, rather than the strength of a covalent bond inside an isolated molecule.

Macroscopic evidence often supports a family of models rather than uniquely identifying a substance. Conductivity depends on charge-carrier concentration and mobility; melting behaviour also depends on the type and extent of structure. Graphite and diamond illustrate why the same element can have different properties when its atoms are arranged and bonded differently. Do not treat a useful classroom model as a universal exception-free rule: some materials have intermediate or temperature-dependent behaviour. A strong response identifies the observed property, names the relevant particles, describes their arrangement or movement, and links that microscopic mechanism to the observation. Additional tests should be chosen because they distinguish competing explanations, not merely because they produce more data.

## Testing a structure explanation against two states

A conductivity claim needs both a charged carrier and a reason that carrier can move. An ionic solid contains charged ions, yet its lattice prevents the ions from moving freely through the material. Melting changes that mobility without turning the ions into neutral atoms. The same evidence should be checked against an alternative model: a typical simple molecular substance has no mobile charged particles even when molten. Conductivity alone is not a unique fingerprint, so combine observations with melting behaviour and composition when available. In an exam explanation, connect the observed property to the particle model explicitly rather than stopping at the label ionic or covalent. Do not describe an ionic lattice as separate molecules.

## Worked example 1

Explain why solid sodium chloride conducts poorly but molten sodium chloride conducts.

1. Identify ions as the charged particles.

2. In the solid, lattice positions restrict their motion through the sample.

3. In the melt, the ions can move and transport charge.

Answer: The change in ion mobility explains the change in conductivity.

## Worked example 2

Why is the statement 'covalent substances melt easily' unreliable?

1. Separate molecular structures from extended covalent networks.

2. Molecular melting can overcome intermolecular attractions without breaking internal covalent bonds.

3. Network substances such as diamond have strong bonding extending throughout the structure.

Answer: The type and extent of structure, not the word covalent alone, determine melting behaviour.

## Worked example 3

Compare diamond and graphite as evidence that elemental identity alone does not determine conductivity.

1. Both are forms of carbon, so a difference in elemental identity cannot explain the contrast.

2. Diamond's extended bonding localises electrons; graphite has delocalised electrons associated with its layered structure.

3. Relate the availability of mobile electrons to graphite's conduction within layers.

Answer: Different bonding and structures can produce different electrical behaviour in forms of the same element.

## Worked example 4

An idealised substance does not conduct as a solid but does conduct when molten. Explain how an ionic model accounts for both observations.

1. The ionic model supplies charged positive and negative ions in both states.

2. In the solid, the ions cannot migrate through the lattice to carry a current.

3. When molten, mobile ions carry charge through the liquid.

Answer: Its ions are fixed in the solid lattice but mobile in the liquid, where they carry charge.

## Misconceptions

- Molten ionic compounds conduct because they release free electrons like metals.
  Correction: Their mobile ions carry charge; metallic conduction is explained by delocalised electrons.

- Boiling water breaks water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen.
  Correction: Ordinary boiling separates molecules from one another; it is not that chemical decomposition.
