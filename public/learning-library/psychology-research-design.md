# Operational definitions, experimental control and inference

## From constructs to measurements

Psychological constructs such as memory, attention or confidence are not identical to any one measurement. An operational definition specifies how a variable is manipulated or measured in a particular study. Counting correctly recalled words after a delay is one possible memory measure; it does not capture every form of memory. A useful definition makes procedures interpretable and repeatable while acknowledging the part of the construct it leaves out. Distinguish a hypothesis about a relationship from the particular task chosen to test it. A convenient measure is not automatically a valid measure of the intended construct.

## Control and assignment

In an experiment, a researcher manipulates an independent variable and measures a dependent variable while trying to control alternative explanations. A confounding variable changes systematically with the manipulation and could explain the observed outcome. Random assignment allocates recruited participants to conditions and can reduce systematic pre-existing differences between those conditions. Random sampling concerns how participants are selected from a population; it addresses a different question about representativeness. Neither procedure guarantees that every small sample is perfectly balanced. Describe what was randomised rather than using random as a general synonym for fair.

## Reliability and validity

Reliability concerns consistency of a measurement or procedure under relevant conditions. Validity concerns whether an interpretation or use is justified, including whether the measure captures the intended construct. A consistently miscalibrated scale illustrates how consistency can coexist with inaccuracy. Internal validity concerns whether an observed effect can be attributed to the proposed cause within the study; generalising beyond the study raises additional questions about people, settings, tasks and time. Correlation alone does not establish direction of causation or eliminate a common cause. A statistically clear pattern can still have a weak measurement or an unrepresentative sample.

## Ethics and conclusions

Research involving people requires attention to informed participation, privacy, potential harm and the ability to withdraw under the applicable ethical framework. A classroom hypothetical design is not permission to carry out a real study. Ethical safeguards also affect interpretation: participants' expectations and the way a task is presented may influence behaviour. A conclusion should name the measured outcome and the conditions under which it was observed, rather than replacing a narrow result with a claim about every aspect of human behaviour. Evaluation is strongest when it explains how a specific design limitation could change the result and proposes a feasible, ethically appropriate way to investigate that uncertainty.

## Separating random allocation from representative recruitment

Recruitment determines who enters a study; allocation determines which condition each recruited participant receives. Random allocation can reduce systematic differences between conditions, but it does not make a convenience sample representative of a wider population. Keep internal comparisons and generalisation separate when evaluating a study. Also operationalise the outcome: a score on a specified recall task is not every aspect of memory. If a fictional study uses volunteers from one club, describe that restriction instead of assuming all learners respond alike. Replication with different participants and settings can investigate generality. The example is a design exercise, not a report of a conducted experiment.

## Separate design control from measurement quality

Random allocation helps distribute participant differences across conditions, but it does not guarantee identical groups or representative recruitment. Repeated-measures designs compare conditions within participants and can reduce some individual differences, while introducing order effects such as practice and fatigue. Counterbalancing distributes those order effects across conditions; it does not remove every possible carryover effect. A reliable measure can repeatedly capture the wrong construct, so explain both what is recorded and why that observation addresses the intended question. These fictional classroom designs are exercises in reasoning, not instructions to run unreviewed human-subject studies.

## Worked example 1

A study defines memory as the number of words recalled after ten minutes. Identify the operational measure and a limitation.

1. The observed variable is the count of recalled words after the specified delay.

2. The broad construct is memory.

3. Other forms of memory and other delays are not directly measured.

Answer: A delayed word-recall score operationalises one aspect of memory, not memory in every context.

## Worked example 2

Volunteers are randomly allocated to two instruction conditions. Does this make the sample representative of all students?

1. Random allocation concerns conditions after recruitment.

2. Recruitment still selected volunteers.

3. Volunteer characteristics may differ from those of the wider population.

Answer: No. Random assignment and representative population sampling are different design features.

## Worked example 3

A study finds that more reported stress accompanies fewer reported hours of sleep. Explain the causal limitation.

1. The result is an association between two measures.

2. Either direction of influence or a third factor could contribute.

3. The association alone does not identify a unique causal mechanism.

Answer: The relationship does not by itself establish which variable causes the other.

## Worked example 4

A fictional study recruits 20 volunteers from one chess club, then randomly assigns 10 to quiet study and 10 to background music. Explain one benefit of allocation and one recruitment limitation.

1. Condition assignment is random among the recruited participants.

2. This helps distribute participant characteristics between conditions without guaranteeing perfect balance.

3. Recruitment from one self-selected club limits population generalisation.

Answer: Random allocation can reduce systematic group differences; the club volunteer sample may not represent the wider learner population.

## Misconceptions

- Random assignment guarantees a representative sample.
  Correction: Assignment allocates participants to conditions; sampling determines who enters the study.

- A reliable measure is automatically valid for every purpose.
  Correction: Consistency alone does not establish that the intended construct or interpretation is captured.

## Flashcards

### What does an operational definition specify?
How a variable is measured or manipulated in the particular study.

### What makes a variable a possible confound?
It varies systematically with the proposed cause and offers an alternative explanation of the outcome.

### What question does random assignment address?
How recruited participants are allocated to conditions, helping reduce systematic group differences.

### What question does sampling address?
How the participants are selected from the population of interest.

### Why can a consistent measure still be unsuitable?
It may reliably measure something other than the intended construct.

### Name two alternatives to a claimed direction of causation in a correlation.
Reverse direction and a common third cause.

### What does random allocation control?
Assignment of recruited participants to conditions; it can reduce systematic group differences.

### Does random allocation guarantee representativeness?
No. Representativeness depends on recruitment, the sampling frame and participation patterns.

## Practice 3 — standard · 2 marks

Why might testing one instruction condition in the morning and the other late at night create a confound?

Answer: Time of day changes systematically with condition and could affect performance, so differences cannot be attributed uniquely to the instructions.

1. Identify the variable changing alongside the instruction condition.

2. Explain its possible alternative influence on the measured result.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify time of day as a potential confound.

- Link it to the uncertainty in causal attribution.

## Practice 4 — extension · 3 marks

Evaluate the claim that an association between revision time and examination marks proves that extra revision causes higher marks.

Answer: The association is compatible with a causal effect, but motivation, prior knowledge or other factors could influence both variables. The observational relationship alone does not isolate the effect of changing revision time.

1. Describe what the association establishes.

2. Identify a plausible common factor.

3. Limit the causal conclusion to what the design supports.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Distinguish association from intervention.

- Identify a plausible confounding explanation.

- Give a qualified conclusion.

## Practice 5 — extension · 3 marks

A self-report questionnaire gives similar scores a week apart. What can and cannot be concluded about the instrument?

Answer: The result supports consistency over that interval in that sample. It does not alone establish construct validity, freedom from response bias or applicability to different populations.

1. Identify the consistency evidence.

2. State that validity requires additional justification.

3. Limit generalisation to the tested conditions and sample.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Describe the bounded reliability evidence.

- Distinguish validity from consistency.

- Identify a remaining limitation.

## Practice 8 — standard · 3 marks

In a fictional within-participant task, everyone completes silence first and music second. Scores improve in the music condition. Give a confound and describe counterbalancing for 20 participants.

Answer: Practice may improve second-task scores. Assign 10 participants to silence then music and 10 to music then silence, preferably randomly assigning order.

1. Identify that condition and order are confounded.

2. Distribute each condition across first and second positions.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify practice or fatigue as an order effect.

- Specify both orders with 10 participants each.

- Explain how the arrangement addresses order confounding.

## Practice 9 — extension · 3 marks

A fictional 'attention' task records how many printed words a student reads aloud in one minute. Explain two other abilities that can affect the score and a consequence for interpreting it.

Answer: Reading fluency and speaking speed can affect the count, so it is not a pure measure of attention.

1. Link each alternative ability directly to the recorded word count.

2. Use additional measures or a design controlling these demands before making a broad attention claim.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify reading fluency.

- Identify speech speed or another distinct relevant factor.

- Explain the construct-interpretation limit.

## Practice 10 — standard · 3 marks

In a fictional experiment, an assessor knows which participants received the preferred instruction and awards subjective participation scores. Name a possible bias and one improvement.

Answer: Observer expectancy could influence scores. Use an assessor unaware of condition and a pre-specified scoring rubric.

1. Knowledge of the expected result may affect subjective judgement.

2. Reduce that information and make scoring criteria explicit.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify observer expectancy.

- Propose assessor blinding.
