# Conservation of mass, formula masses and measurement uncertainty

## Objectives

## Define the system before comparing masses

In an ordinary chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged into different combinations. If every reactant and product is included, the total mass is conserved to the accuracy of school chemical measurements. A system boundary identifies what is being counted. A sealed container and its entire contents form a different measured system from an open dish containing only the remaining solid. State the boundary before declaring that a mass observation agrees or disagrees with a model.

Gas matters even when it cannot be seen. A solid may gain mass when atoms from a gas enter it, or a reaction vessel may lose measured contents when gas leaves. Neither observation requires atoms to appear from nowhere or vanish. The useful bookkeeping is initial included mass plus incoming mass minus outgoing mass equals final included mass. This is an account of matter crossing a boundary, not a claim that every laboratory mass difference is entirely due to gas; splashes, evaporation and measurement errors may also contribute.

## Coefficients count formula units; subscripts identify substances

A chemical formula fixes the composition of a substance. In H₂O, the subscript 2 specifies two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom. A coefficient in front multiplies the entire formula: 3H₂O represents six hydrogen atoms and three oxygen atoms. When balancing a chemical equation, change coefficients rather than subscripts. Changing H₂O to H₂O₂ would replace water with a different substance, so it would no longer be the same reaction.

Use a count table for each element on the left and right. For 2Al+3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃, the two sides each contain two aluminium atoms and six chlorine atoms. Check every element after each adjustment because changing a coefficient can affect more than one count. Use the simplest whole-number coefficient ratio when requested. A balanced equation conserves atoms but does not by itself show reaction speed, experimental yield or that arbitrary conditions will make the reaction occur. Those are separate chemical questions.

## Formula mass and percentage composition

Relative formula mass is found by adding the supplied relative atomic masses for every atom represented in one formula unit. Brackets require multiplication: Mg(OH)₂ contains one magnesium, two oxygens and two hydrogens. Using Mg=24, O=16 and H=1 gives 24+2×(16+1)=58. Do not multiply only the final atom in the bracket. The resulting relative formula mass is a ratio and has no unit; a measured mass of a chemical sample has units such as grams.

The percentage by mass of an element equals its total relative-mass contribution divided by the relative formula mass, multiplied by 100. In the same example, oxygen contributes 32 of the total 58. Count atoms first, then convert to mass contributions: a fraction of atoms is generally not the same as a fraction of mass because different atoms have different masses. If percentages for all constituent elements are calculated consistently, they should sum to approximately 100%, with small differences possible from rounding. Retain extra digits until the final answer.

## Open systems and apparent mass changes

Consider magnesium combining with oxygen to form magnesium oxide. Comparing only the starting magnesium with the final oxide omits a reactant: oxygen from the air. A greater final solid mass is therefore expected if oxygen has joined the solid and none of the product has been lost. The mass increase can estimate the incorporated oxygen under those stated assumptions. It does not imply that burning manufactures matter.

Conversely, calcium carbonate can decompose to calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. If carbon dioxide escapes from an open vessel, the remaining solid weighs less than the initial carbonate. Adding the escaped gas to the final accounting restores the mass balance. A mass change alone does not identify the gas; the reaction model or other evidence is needed. These examples explain results rather than prescribe experiments. Heating, reactive materials and pressure require a supervised school procedure and risk assessment. In particular, this lesson must not be read as an instruction to heat a sealed vessel.

## Repeat readings, uncertainty and suitable precision

Measurements have uncertainty. Repeated values can show how much results vary under similar conditions, and their arithmetic mean is a useful summary when the data are appropriate. The range is maximum minus minimum. Some classroom questions explicitly use half the range as an uncertainty estimate around the mean; use that convention only when stated or justified. It is not a universal definition of uncertainty and does not automatically include every source of error.

Closely grouped readings demonstrate repeatability, not necessarily closeness to the true value. A balance with a constant offset can produce similar readings that are all biased. Repeating the same procedure may expose random variation without removing that offset. A small set of repeat values can also underestimate variability. Explain whether an improvement addresses repeated scatter, instrument resolution, a zero error or loss of material. Report enough digits to make the result useful without inventing precision that the measurement method cannot support.

## Build an answer that can be checked

For an equation, show both atom counts and leave each formula unchanged. For a formula mass, write the atom inventory before doing arithmetic. For a mass-change explanation, name the gas or other material that crosses the measurement boundary and identify whether it enters or leaves. For repeated measurements, give the actual values used, state any convention for estimating uncertainty, and include the unit. These different tasks need different evidence; one correct number is not a substitute for every explanation.

A useful diagnostic routine asks four questions: Have all relevant substances been counted? Have coefficients and subscripts been kept distinct? Does the percentage have a plausible value between zero and one hundred? Does the uncertainty claim match what the data actually show? If a calculation conflicts with conservation, revisit omitted gases and lost product before rejecting the model. If data cannot distinguish competing causes, say so. Remaining study should include broader equation balancing, graphing measurement distributions and supervised practical interpretation; the present tasks are not a complete quantitative-chemistry course.

## Explaining a mass gain in an open reaction system

A mass comparison needs an explicit system boundary. When a solid reacts with oxygen from the air, the measured solid can gain mass because matter crosses into the measured system. This is consistent with conservation for a larger system including the air. Conversely, a gas leaving an open container can reduce the measured mass of its contents. Record which objects are on the balance, whether the container is closed and whether material was lost during handling. In a calculation, the mass difference can represent oxygen gained only under assumptions such as complete reaction, no other reactants entering and no product loss. State those assumptions instead of treating every increase as oxygen uptake.

## A measurement model must include the vessel and its uncertainty

Mass comparisons require a consistent system boundary and a clear tare procedure. A vessel's mass is subtracted from the combined reading to obtain the sample mass. If the same stable additive balance offset affects both readings, it can cancel in the difference, though it would still bias either absolute reading. This does not remove all uncertainty: resolution, drift, sample loss and changed conditions may remain. When uncertainty rules are supplied in a problem, use those rules explicitly instead of treating one classroom convention as universal.

## Worked example 1

Balance Al+Cl₂ → AlCl₃ using smallest whole-number coefficients.

1. Two AlCl₃ units contain six chlorine atoms.

2. Use three Cl₂ molecules and two Al atoms.

3. Recount both elements.

Answer: 2Al+3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃.

## Worked example 2

Find Mᵣ of Al₂(SO₄)₃ using Al=27, S=32 and O=16.

1. There are two Al, three S and twelve O atoms.

2. Calculate 2×27+3×32+12×16.

Answer: 342.

## Worked example 3

Using Ca=40, C=12 and O=16, find the oxygen percentage by mass in CaCO₃.

1. Formula mass is 40+12+3×16=100.

2. Oxygen contributes 48.

3. Calculate 48/100×100.

Answer: 48%.

## Worked example 4

A solid gains 0.80 g while reacting with oxygen; all solid is retained. Explain.

1. The measured solid is an open system for oxygen.

2. The extra solid mass corresponds to oxygen entering, under the assumptions.

Answer: 0.80 g of oxygen is incorporated; total matter is not created.

## Worked example 5

Results are 2.36, 2.40 and 2.44 g. Use half-range as the stated uncertainty estimate.

1. Mean is (2.36+2.40+2.44)/3=2.40 g.

2. Range is 0.08 g; half-range is 0.04 g.

3. This repeat-based estimate does not eliminate calibration bias.

Answer: 2.40±0.04 g under the stated classroom convention.

## Worked example 6

In an idealised reaction, 2.40 g of metal reacts completely with oxygen to form 4.00 g of oxide, with no material lost. Find the oxygen mass gained and its percentage of the oxide mass.

1. Conservation within the stated reaction gives oxygen mass 4.00 - 2.40 = 1.60 g.

2. For composition of the oxide, use total oxide mass as denominator.

3. 1.60/4.00 × 100 = 40%.

Answer: Oxygen gained: 1.60 g. Oxygen is 40% of the oxide mass.

## Misconceptions

- Balancing permits changing the subscript in a chemical formula.
  Correction: Change coefficients; changing a subscript changes substance identity.

- A lighter final solid disproves conservation of mass.
  Correction: A gaseous product or other material may have left the measured system.

- Repeatable readings must be accurate.
  Correction: A common bias can affect every repeat.

## Flashcards

### Why define a system boundary before interpreting a mass change?
It establishes which entering or leaving materials were included in the measurement.

### What does a coefficient of 3 in 3H₂O multiply?
The entire formula: six H atoms and three O atoms.

### Why must subscripts stay fixed when balancing an equation?
They specify the chemical identity of each substance.

### How many oxygen atoms are represented by Al₂(SO₄)₃?
Twelve.

### How is relative formula mass calculated?
Add the relative atomic masses of every atom counted in the formula.

### Does relative formula mass carry a gram unit?
No. It is relative; sample mass in grams is a different quantity.

### What is the percentage-by-mass formula for one element?
Its total relative-mass contribution divided by Mᵣ, multiplied by 100.

### Why can a metal's solid product gain mass in air?
Atoms from a gaseous reactant can enter the measured solid.

### Why can a carbonate's remaining solid lose mass on decomposition?
A gaseous product can leave the measured system.

### How is the range of repeat readings found?
Maximum minus minimum.

### What does close agreement among repeats establish directly?
Repeatability under the tested conditions, not guaranteed accuracy.

### When should half-range be used as an uncertainty estimate?
When that convention is stated or justified, with its limitations explained.

### Why can a reacting solid gain mass in air?
It can incorporate matter such as oxygen from outside the initially measured solid.

### Which denominator gives oxygen percentage in an oxide?
The total oxide mass, not just the starting metal mass.
