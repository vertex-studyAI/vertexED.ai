## A model is an explanation constrained by evidence

An atomic model represents features that help us explain observations; it is not a magnified photograph. Early indivisible-sphere pictures could not account for evidence that atoms contain negatively charged electrons. A model with electrons embedded in distributed positive charge addressed electrical neutrality but made different scattering predictions from a model with charge concentrated in a tiny nucleus. The useful question is not simply which model came later, but which observations a model can and cannot explain.

In the alpha-particle scattering account used here, most particles pass through the thin foil with little deflection, while a very small fraction are strongly deflected. The combination supports a mostly empty atomic volume with positive charge and much of the mass concentrated in a compact centre. A few large deflections are especially informative: a model must explain those as well as the common straight paths. The experiment did not directly show electron shells, and it did not by itself establish the neutron. Those features required additional evidence.

## Particle bookkeeping and electrical charge

A proton has relative charge +1 and a neutron has charge 0. An electron has relative charge −1. Protons and neutrons each have relative mass approximately 1, while an electron is much lighter. Proton number Z identifies the element. If a species contains 12 protons, it is magnesium regardless of how many electrons it has. Changing the proton number changes the element; removing an electron does not. Chemical ion formation rearranges electrons without changing the nucleus in this model.

For a neutral atom, the electron count equals Z because the charges cancel. For an ion with signed charge q measured in elementary-charge units, electron count is Z−q. A 2+ ion therefore has two fewer electrons than protons; a 1− ion has one more. Write the sign before substituting: subtracting a negative charge adds an electron. A useful check is to reconstruct net charge as protons minus electrons. Never subtract the charge from the neutron count, because neutrons carry no electrical charge.

## Isotopes, mass numbers and physical scale

Mass number A counts the protons and neutrons in one nucleus, so neutron count is A−Z. Isotopes share Z but differ in neutron count. For example, a hypothetical sample of magnesium-24 and magnesium-26 contains nuclei with the same 12 protons but different neutron totals. The element identity is unchanged. An isotope can also be ionised: isotope identity and ion charge answer different questions, so a species can differ from another in both neutrons and electrons.

Atomic dimensions need powers of ten rather than ordinary whole-number drawings. A radius of 0.10 nm equals 1.0×10⁻¹⁰ m because nano means one billionth. The nucleus is vastly smaller than the atom, and ordinary shell sketches are not to scale. Most atomic mass is associated with the nucleus even though the nucleus occupies very little volume. Keep radius comparisons separate from volume comparisons: multiplying a radius by a factor multiplies the volume of a similar sphere by the cube of that factor. No exact universal nuclear radius is assumed here.

## Why relative atomic mass can be non-integer

Relative atomic mass describes an abundance-weighted mean on the relative atomic-mass scale, not the nucleon count of a particular atom. In classroom isotope calculations, the supplied isotope masses are often approximated by integer mass numbers. If 60% of a fictional sample has mass 10 and 40% has mass 11, the mean is 0.60×10+0.40×11=10.4. There is no need to invent an atom with 10.4 nucleons. The average describes a collection.

First check that abundances use a consistent basis and sum to the full sample. With percentages, divide the weighted sum by 100; with counts, divide by the total count. The result must lie between the smallest and largest isotope masses, and it should be closer to the more abundant mass. An unweighted mean is appropriate only for equal abundances. Relative atomic mass has no unit; a sample mass in grams is a different quantity. Real high-precision isotope masses are not always exactly the mass numbers, so retain supplied data and avoid claiming unnecessary precision.

## Electron shells as a bounded school representation

For the selected ground-state atoms among the first twenty elements, electron arrangements can be written as numbers in successive occupied shells. Sodium has 11 electrons arranged 2,8,1; chlorine has 17 arranged 2,8,7; calcium has 20 arranged 2,8,8,2. Count the entries to check the total. A drawing uses a nucleus and labelled shells with the same electron numbers. The drawn circles are a representation of energy levels, not literal tracks measured in the scattering experiment.

An ion requires a fresh electron count. Sodium forming Na⁺ loses its outer electron and is represented as 2,8. Chlorine forming Cl⁻ gains an electron and is represented as 2,8,8. Neither process changes the proton number. The simple first-twenty pattern must not be extended into a claim that every shell can hold only eight electrons. Later chemistry uses subshells and orbitals to account for more complicated configurations. This lesson uses the bounded school model because it supports the named task, while keeping that limitation explicit.

## A reliable solution and revision routine

Start a particle-count question by listing the information separately: element or Z, isotope mass number A, and charge q. Then calculate protons Z, neutrons A−Z, and electrons Z−q. Check that counts are non-negative whole numbers and that protons minus electrons reproduces the charge. If a question supplies only a periodic-table relative atomic mass, do not silently round it and pretend that a unique isotope was specified. Additional isotope information may be needed to determine the neutron count.

For an explanation, connect each statement to what it supports. A mostly empty volume explains the frequent low-deflection observations; concentrated positive charge explains rare large deflections. For an isotope average, show weights and a sensible-range check. For a shell diagram, show the correct electron total and identify whether the species is neutral. A complete revision session alternates these skills instead of memorising only definitions. Remaining work includes broader element recall, periodic trends and more diagram construction; successful answers here do not establish completion of the whole atomic-structure unit.

## Inferring an ion from particle counts

Atomic number identifies an element through its proton count. An ion retains that identity when electrons are transferred; changing protons would change the element. Work from the signed difference between protons and electrons to find the charge, and use protons plus neutrons for the mass number. These are counts for one specified isotope, not a naturally occurring weighted relative atomic mass. A shell drawing can represent the electron count but does not replace the particle accounting. For the first twenty elements, the simplified shell model is useful within its school-level scope. It should not be used to infer that every atom fills shells according to an unrestricted 2,8,8 pattern.

## Worked example 1

An ion has Z=13, A=27 and charge 3+. Find its particle counts.

1. Protons are fixed by Z: 13.

2. Neutrons are 27−13=14.

3. Electrons are 13−3=10; check 13−10=3.

Answer: 13 protons, 14 neutrons and 10 electrons.

## Worked example 2

A fictional element has 80 atoms of isotope mass 20 and 20 atoms of isotope mass 22. Find its mean relative mass.

1. Use counts as weights: 80×20+20×22=2040.

2. Divide by the total 100 atoms.

3. Check that the mean lies between 20 and 22, closer to 20.

Answer: 20.4, with no unit.

## Worked example 3

Compare neutral chlorine-35 and chloride-35, both with Z=17.

1. Both nuclei contain 17 protons and 35−17=18 neutrons.

2. The neutral atom has 17 electrons; the 1− ion has 18.

3. Write the school shell arrangements as 2,8,7 and 2,8,8.

Answer: Ion formation changes electrons, not isotope identity.

## Worked example 4

Convert an atomic radius of 0.12 nm into metres.

1. One nanometre is 10⁻⁹ metres.

2. Multiply 0.12 by 10⁻⁹ and put it in standard form.

Answer: 1.2×10⁻¹⁰ m.

## Worked example 5

Why does a small minority of strongly deflected alpha particles matter?

1. A satisfactory model must account for rare observations as well as common ones.

2. Large deflections support concentrated charge rather than only a diffuse positive background.

Answer: They discriminate between competing charge-distribution models; they do not establish electron shells by themselves.

## Worked example 6

A particle has 13 protons, 14 neutrons and 10 electrons. Give its atomic number, mass number and charge, explaining each.

1. Atomic number equals the 13 protons.

2. Mass number counts protons and neutrons: 13 + 14 = 27.

3. There are three more protons than electrons, giving a charge of 3+.

Answer: Atomic number 13; mass number 27; charge 3+.

## Misconceptions

- An ion is a different element because its charge changed.
  Correction: The element is fixed by proton number, not electron count.

- An isotope mean of 24.5 means each nucleus contains half a neutron.
  Correction: The mean describes abundances across a sample, not the particle count in one nucleus.

- The first-twenty shell pattern proves every shell holds at most eight electrons.
  Correction: It is a bounded representation, not a universal shell-capacity rule.

## Flashcards

### Which particle count fixes element identity?
The proton number Z.

### How many electrons does a neutral atom have?
The same number as its protons.

### How do you obtain signed ion charge from particle counts?
Protons minus electrons, in elementary-charge units.

### What is the neutron count for mass number A and proton number Z?
A−Z.

### What differs between isotopes of one element?
Neutron count; proton number remains fixed.

### What changes when an atom forms an ion through ordinary electron transfer?
Electron count, not the nucleus.

### Why can relative atomic mass be non-integer?
It is an abundance-weighted mean of isotope masses.

### What is wrong with averaging isotope masses without weights?
It assumes equal abundance, which may not match the sample.

### What does mostly low-deflection alpha scattering support?
Most atomic volume contains no concentrated obstacle to the particles.

### What do rare strong alpha deflections support?
Positive charge concentrated in a small region.

### What is the first-twenty shell representation of Cl⁻?
2,8,8; there are 18 electrons.

### What conversion connects nanometres to metres?
1 nm=10⁻⁹ m.

### Does forming an ion by electron transfer change its mass number?
No. Mass number counts protons and neutrons, which are unchanged by electron transfer.

### How is ionic charge found from particle counts?
Subtract the electron count from the proton count, expressing the result as a signed charge in elementary-charge units.
