## Both tiers: square and cube recall

A positive integer power counts repeated factors, not repeated addition: 3⁴=3×3×3×3=81. Recall squares from 1² to 15²: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169, 196, 225. The corresponding square roots reverse these pairs. Recall cubes of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 10: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 1000. Build a forgotten value from multiplication rather than guessing.

The notation √49 means the nonnegative principal root, 7. The equation x²=49 has two solutions, 7 and −7. Odd roots preserve signs, so ∛(−125)=−5. A fourth root asks which nonnegative value has the given fourth power: the fourth root of 81 is 3. Real square roots of negative numbers are not available in these Number topics.

## Both tiers: index laws and their conditions

For a common nonzero base a, multiplication adds indices and division subtracts them: aᵐaⁿ=aᵐ⁺ⁿ and aᵐ/aⁿ=aᵐ⁻ⁿ. A power of a power multiplies indices: (aᵐ)ⁿ=aᵐⁿ. A power applies to every factor in a bracket, so (2x)³=8x³. These laws do not combine unlike bases by adding indices: 2³×3² is 72, not 6⁵.

The quotient a³/a³ equals 1, which motivates a⁰=1 for a≠0. A negative index means a reciprocal: a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ. It does not make a number negative: 4⁻²=1/16. The base and brackets still matter: (−2)⁴=16 but −2⁴=−16. Do not use these division rules at zero or assume an expression containing 0⁰ is defined.

## Higher / Extended: fractional indices

For positive a, a^(1/n) is the positive nth root, and a^(m/n) is that root raised to m. Thus 27^(2/3)=(∛27)²=9. For a negative fractional exponent, evaluate the positive power and take its reciprocal: 16^(−3/4)=1/(2³)=1/8. Simplifying the fraction in an exponent first can make the root easier. Restricting to positive bases avoids ambiguous real-power conventions involving negative numbers and even roots.

Fractional indices obey the same laws for positive bases: x^(1/2)×x^(3/2)=x². Read 9^(1/2) as a root, not 9 divided by 2. Show the root and power stages in non-calculator questions so the exponent has a mathematical meaning instead of being only a calculator instruction.

## GCSE Higher: estimate powers and roots

To estimate √50, bracket it between consecutive known squares: 7²=49 and 8²=64, so 7<√50<8 and it lies much nearer 7. Testing 7.1²=50.41 shows √50<7.1. Similarly 4³=64 and 5³=125 locate ∛90 between 4 and 5. An estimate is not an exact equality; write an approximation sign and give only supported precision. This bracketing method also checks a calculator result and detects an incorrect root key.

## Worked example 1

Simplify (3⁵×3⁻²)/3² and evaluate 5⁻².

1. For the first expression the total exponent is 5−2−2=1.

2. A negative exponent denotes a reciprocal, so 5⁻²=1/5².

Answer: 3 and 1/25.

## Worked example 2

[Higher / Extended] Evaluate 81^(3/4) and 32^(−2/5).

1. The fourth root of 81 is 3, then 3³=27.

2. The fifth root of 32 is 2; square to get 4, then take the reciprocal.

Answer: 27 and 1/4.

## Misconceptions

- A negative index gives a negative number.
  Correction: It gives a reciprocal. For example 2⁻³=1/8.

- √36 is ±6.
  Correction: The symbol denotes 6; x²=36 has the two solutions ±6.

## Practice 1 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Without a calculator, evaluate 14², √144, 4³, ∛1000 and the fourth root of 625.

Answer: 196, 12, 64, 10, 5.

1. Use square and cube pairs for the first four.

2. 5²=25 and 25²=625 establish the fourth root.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Give the four square/cube results.

- Justify the fourth root as 5.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] State √121 and all real solutions of x²=121. Also evaluate ∛(−64).

Answer: 11; x=11 or −11; −4.

1. The root symbol chooses the nonnegative square root.

2. Squaring either sign gives 121, while (−4)³=−64.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Distinguish the root from both equation solutions.

- Give the signed cube root.

## Practice 3 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Simplify (2⁷×2⁻³)/2² and (5²)³÷5⁶.

Answer: 4 and 1.

1. The first exponent is 7−3−2=2.

2. The second exponent is 2×3−6=0 and the base is nonzero.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Correctly combine the first exponents.

- Use the zero power rule in the second.

## Practice 4 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Evaluate 10⁻³, (−3)⁻² and −3². Explain the differing signs.

Answer: 0.001, 1/9, −9.

1. A negative exponent creates a reciprocal; squaring −3 gives positive 9.

2. In −3² the minus sign is outside the square.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Give all three values.

- Explain exponent sign versus base sign.

## Practice 5 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Evaluate 2⁵ and the fifth root of 243. Check your root using multiplication.

Answer: 32 and 3; 3×3×3×3×3=243.

1. Five factors of 2 give 32.

2. Five factors of 3 give 243, reversing the root operation.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Give 32.

- Give and check the fifth root.

## Practice 6 — extension · 2 marks

[GCSE Higher; IGCSE Extended] Evaluate 64^(2/3) and 16^(−1/2) exactly.

Answer: 16 and 1/4.

1. ∛64=4 and 4²=16.

2. √16=4; the negative index gives its reciprocal.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Show the root then square for 16.

- Show the reciprocal root for 1/4.

## Practice 7 — extension · 2 marks

[GCSE Higher; IGCSE Extended] For x>0 simplify x^(5/2)÷x^(1/2), and evaluate 8^(4/3).

Answer: x² and 16.

1. Subtract the exponents: 5/2−1/2=2.

2. ∛8=2, then 2⁴=16.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Obtain x² using a valid index law.

- Evaluate the fractional power as 16.

## Practice 8 — extension · 2 marks

[GCSE Higher] Without a calculator show that √70 lies between 8.3 and 8.4.

Answer: 8.3²=68.89<70<70.56=8.4², so 8.3<√70<8.4.

1. Square both positive trial values.

2. Use that squaring increases with a positive input to reverse the comparison safely.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Compute both trial squares.

- State the justified interval.
