## Prerequisites

- Distinguish an author from a character.

- Identify a passage's speaker and situation.

## Objectives

- Distinguish narrative statement from established fictional fact.

- Support an interpretation through form and detail.

- Compare two passages using a focused argument.

## Separate author, narrator and character

A narrator is the voice presenting a story and is not automatically identical to its author. A character's statement may reveal that character's belief rather than establish a fact about the fictional world. First-person narration can give access to a viewpoint while limiting access to other minds and events. Ask what the narrator could know, what is directly observed, and what is inferred. Calling a narrator unreliable requires evidence of limits, contradiction or distortion; it is not simply another name for a narrator whose opinions a reader dislikes.

## Read action and form together

In drama, a pause, entrance, exit or stage direction can complicate what a character says. In poetry, line breaks, sound patterns and stanza organisation can shape emphasis, but no formal feature has a single guaranteed meaning. Track contrasts between speech and action or between stated confidence and hesitant form. Explain how the particular choice contributes to the passage's tension or argument. Avoid importing a plot summary that is not needed for the question. Close attention to a few details often supports a more coherent interpretation than a catalogue of devices.

## Build an argument rather than a checklist

A literary paragraph should advance a claim and examine the evidence that makes it plausible. A competing reading can refine the claim instead of being mentioned only to dismiss it. When comparing passages, organise around a shared issue such as power, memory or belonging, then explain a significant difference in its treatment. Use verified contextual information only where it illuminates the text and task. ## Worked example 1

Original fictional narration: 'Everyone welcomed me, I decided, although no one looked up.' Discuss the tension.

1. The narrator's confident interpretation conflicts with the observed lack of response.

2. This invites doubt about the interpretation without proving the others' actual feelings.

Answer: The sentence exposes a gap between self-assurance and observable evidence.

## Worked example 2

Original stage direction: '[Mara locks the drawer.] MARA: I have nothing to hide.' Explain the contrast.

1. Locking the drawer creates a possible conflict with the spoken claim.

2. The audience may suspect concealment, although privacy is another possible explanation.

Answer: Action complicates the claim and creates interpretive tension.

## Misconceptions

- A narrator's interpretation must be accepted as fact.
  Correction: Compare the interpretation with what the text actually shows.

- Context can replace close textual evidence.
  Correction: Relevant context supports interpretation; it does not substitute for evidence.

## Flashcards

### Is the narrator necessarily the author?
No. The narrator is a constructed voice within the work.

### What can a character's statement establish directly?
That the character says or believes it, not necessarily that it is true.

### Why analyse stage directions?
Action and staging can qualify or contradict spoken words.

### How should a comparative paragraph be organised?
Around a focused shared issue and evidence from both passages.

### Does this lesson replace a prescribed text?

## Practice 1 — standard · 2 marks

Original fictional narration: 'I knew he regretted leaving; he had not written once.' What is observed, and what is inferred?

Answer: The lack of a letter is reported; regret is inferred by the narrator.

1. Separate the reported behaviour from the mental-state claim.

2. Note that several motives could explain not writing.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify the observation.

- Identify the inference.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

Original stage direction: '[Tomas steps away from the open door.] TOMAS: I am ready.' Explain one possible tension.

Answer: His movement away may suggest hesitation despite his claim of readiness.

1. Compare the direction of movement with the spoken declaration.

2. Keep the reading tentative because more context may alter it.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Use action and speech.

- Explain a qualified contrast.

## Practice 3 — extension · 2 marks

A character gives away a coat. One reader calls this generous; another calls it an attempt to impress an audience. What evidence would help distinguish the readings?

Answer: Evidence about the recipient's need, the giver's words and behaviour, and whether an audience is present could help.

1. Identify details relevant to motive rather than assuming a single meaning.

2. Explain how those details might support or weaken each reading.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Name relevant textual evidence.

- Relate it to the competing interpretations.

## Practice 4 — extension · 2 marks

Original images: A calls memory 'a locked room'; B calls it 'an open road'. Propose a comparative claim.

Answer: A presents memory as enclosed or difficult to access, while B presents it as accessible or leading onward.

1. Explain the spatial associations of each image.

2. Compare their implications while acknowledging that context may change them.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Analyse both images.

- Form a qualified comparative claim.
