## Prophecy offers a possibility, not an instruction

In Act 1 the witches predict Macbeth’s rise, and the confirmation of the Cawdor title gives their words apparent credibility. Yet they do not instruct him to murder Duncan. His response therefore opens a question of agency: supernatural encouragement may stimulate desires that he chooses to act upon. Banquo’s caution provides a dramatic contrast to Macbeth’s imaginative investment. Avoid turning the play into a simple equation in which hearing a prophecy removes moral responsibility.

## Soliloquy makes conflict available to the audience

In Act 1 Scene 7 Macbeth considers obligations to Duncan as king, kinsman and guest, as well as the consequences of violence. A soliloquy lets an audience hear reasons for restraint before witnessing their rejection. Lady Macbeth then challenges his courage and masculine identity. Her persuasion is important because it addresses doubts already staged; it does not mean Macbeth has no ambition of his own. Analyse the sequence of thought, pressure and decision rather than collecting isolated quotations about evil.

## A partnership changes after the murder

After Duncan’s death Macbeth becomes preoccupied with security. In Act 3 he arranges Banquo’s murder without giving Lady Macbeth the same role she had in the first plot. The change makes the earlier persuasion an incomplete explanation for later violence. At the banquet the ghost disrupts his public performance of kingship: a ruler who acquired power through secrecy cannot reliably control what the audience sees on stage. Whether the ghost is interpreted psychologically or supernaturally, the public breakdown exposes a gap between authority and self-command.

## Guilt, context and the ending

Lady Macbeth’s sleepwalking in Act 5 contrasts with her earlier confidence about washing away evidence. Repeated gestures turn remembered violence into involuntary performance, reversing the apparent strength of the early partnership. Macbeth’s later bleakness can invite sympathy for suffering without excusing the killings he orders. His defeat and Malcolm’s restoration provide a political ending, but the audience has experienced a sustained examination of desire and responsibility.

Context should illuminate textual choices: a culture concerned with monarchy, treason and supernatural temptation can help explain the stakes of killing a king. Do not claim that every Jacobean spectator held one identical belief or that a historical fact proves a single interpretation. Build paragraphs from a claim, precise scene evidence, a method and an explanation of its significance within the whole play.

## Worked example 1

Improve the claim: The witches make Macbeth kill Duncan.

1. Distinguish prediction from command.

2. Use the soliloquy’s competing reasons as evidence of agency.

Answer: The prophecy encourages Macbeth’s ambition, but his Act 1 Scene 7 deliberation presents murder as a choice made despite recognised obligations.

## Worked example 2

Explain a structural contrast between Lady Macbeth early and late in the play.

1. Locate early post-murder behaviour and Act 5 sleepwalking.

2. Explain how the reversal changes an audience’s understanding of control.

Answer: Her early confidence about removing evidence contrasts with later compulsive handwashing, making guilt resistant to physical cleaning.

## Misconceptions

- A contextual fact is automatically analysis.
  Correction: It becomes useful when it explains a specific dramatic choice and supports the argument.

- Sympathy for Macbeth means approving his actions.
  Correction: A tragedy can expose suffering while retaining moral responsibility.

## Flashcards

### Why is Banquo a useful contrast?
His caution offers an alternative response to prophecy.

### What does the Act 1 Scene 7 soliloquy reveal?
Reasons to resist murder before Macbeth chooses it.

### How does the partnership change in Act 3?
Macbeth arranges further violence more independently.

### What does sleepwalking dramatise?
Guilt returning through involuntary behaviour.

### What makes whole-play evidence useful?
It shows development or contrast rather than an isolated trait.
