## Position, change and direction

Choose a coordinate direction before assigning signs. Displacement is final position minus initial position and can be positive, negative or zero. Distance is the total path length and is nonnegative. Average velocity is displacement divided by elapsed time; average speed uses distance instead. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, so negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down. An object moving in the negative direction speeds up if its acceleration is also negative. State whether a value is an average over an interval or an instantaneous value at one time.

## Graphs and constant-acceleration equations

The gradient of a displacement-time graph gives velocity. The gradient of a velocity-time graph gives acceleration, while its signed area gives displacement. Area below the time axis contributes negatively to displacement but positively to distance when its magnitude is used. The equations v=u+at and s=ut+½at² assume constant acceleration over the interval; applying them to arbitrary changing acceleration is unjustified. A horizontal velocity graph describes constant velocity, not necessarily rest. When using a graph, inspect axis quantities and units before interpreting steepness, because the same line shape has different meanings on different axes.

## Forces act on a chosen object

Newton's second law relates the resultant external force on a constant-mass system to its acceleration: F_net=ma. Add forces as vectors, with signs in one dimension, rather than substituting the largest individual force. A zero resultant force means zero acceleration in an inertial frame; the object may remain at rest or continue at constant velocity. Weight mg is gravitational force, measured in newtons, whereas mass is measured in kilograms. Action-reaction forces have equal magnitude and opposite direction but act on different bodies, so they do not cancel in the free-body diagram of one body. Begin a solution by identifying the body and drawing all external forces acting on it.

## Choosing the system and interpreting a force model

A force diagram represents forces acting on one selected object, not every force present in the scene. If a book rests on a table, the upward normal force and downward weight act on the book. The equal-and-opposite partners required by Newton's third law act on other bodies, so they do not cancel each other on the book's own diagram. Forces that balance on one object need not be a third-law pair.

Choose a positive direction before resolving forces and preserve it in velocity and acceleration signs. A negative acceleration means acceleration opposite the chosen positive direction, not automatically that speed is decreasing. Speed decreases when velocity and acceleration point in opposite directions. Constant-acceleration formulas assume acceleration is constant over the whole interval; they cannot be applied unchanged to arbitrary varying-force motion. Explain the assumptions behind any simplified model, such as negligible drag or constant mass. When a prediction differs from an observation, check measurements, sign choices and omitted interactions before deciding that a physical law has failed.

## Separating stopping time, distance and force direction

Constant deceleration is a signed acceleration opposite to a chosen positive velocity. Set a direction convention before using a motion equation and retain the sign until interpreting the result. The stopping time follows from final velocity zero; the stopping distance can be checked with the mean velocity times time under constant acceleration. Resultant force then uses the mass of the chosen object and the same signed acceleration. A force opposing motion is not necessarily equal to every individual resistive force if other forces act. The model here omits reaction time and assumes constant resultant force, so it should not be presented as a complete real stopping-distance prediction.

## Separate signed displacement from total distance

The area under a velocity-time graph is signed displacement. Motion in the opposite direction contributes negative area, while distance adds the magnitudes of all segments. A zero final displacement can therefore accompany substantial travel. Average speed divides total distance by elapsed time; average velocity divides displacement by the same interval. Force determines acceleration in the constant-mass Newtonian model, not the direction of velocity itself. A nonzero velocity can persist with zero resultant force, and a force opposing velocity can slow an object without immediately reversing it.

## Worked example 1

A cart starts at 2 m/s and accelerates uniformly at 3 m/s² for 4 s. Find its final velocity and displacement.

1. Use v=u+at: 2+3×4=14 m/s.

2. Use s=ut+½at²: 2×4+½×3×16=32 m.

3. Check displacement using average velocity (2+14)/2 times 4.

Answer: 14 m/s and 32 m.

## Worked example 2

A 5 kg crate has forces 18 N right and 8 N left. Find its acceleration.

1. Choose right as positive and sum forces: 18-8=10 N.

2. Divide the resultant by mass: a=10/5.

3. The positive result indicates rightward acceleration.

Answer: 2 m/s² right.

## Worked example 3

A car moves left with velocity -8 m/s and acceleration +2 m/s². Describe its initial change in speed.

1. The signs refer to a positive-right coordinate system.

2. Velocity and acceleration initially point in opposite directions.

3. After one second of constant acceleration, velocity is -6 m/s, with a smaller magnitude.

Answer: The car initially slows down; positive acceleration does not necessarily mean speeding up.

## Worked example 4

A 2 kg trolley moves at +6 m s⁻¹ and has constant acceleration -3 m s⁻² until it stops. Find stopping time, distance and resultant force.

1. Use 0 = 6 - 3t to obtain t = 2 s.

2. Mean velocity is (6 + 0)/2 = 3 m s⁻¹, so distance is 3 × 2 = 6 m.

3. F = ma = 2 × (-3) = -6 N.

Answer: Time 2 s; distance 6 m; resultant force -6 N, opposite to the initial motion.

## Misconceptions

- An object moving right must have a rightward resultant force.
  Correction: Force determines acceleration, not the direction of existing velocity.

- Action-reaction forces cancel on the same object.
  Correction: They act on different objects.

## Flashcards

### When can distance be nonzero but displacement zero?
When an object returns to its starting position.

### What does a velocity-time gradient measure?
Acceleration, with units m/s² when SI axes are used.

### What does signed area under a velocity-time graph measure?
Displacement; integrate speed for total distance.

### Which force belongs in F=ma?
The vector sum of external forces acting on the chosen system.

### Why do third-law pairs not cancel in one object's force balance?
Each member acts on a different body.

### When does acceleration reduce an object's speed?
When acceleration points opposite to the object's instantaneous velocity, regardless of the chosen positive direction.

### How do you find mean velocity under constant acceleration?
Use (initial velocity + final velocity)/2 over the interval.

### What does a negative resultant force mean after choosing a positive direction?
The force acts opposite to that chosen positive direction; the sign is directional, not a negative magnitude.

## Practice 7 — extension · 3 marks

A learner computes 6 × 2 = 12 m as the stopping distance. Explain the assumption behind that calculation and why it fails here.

Answer: It assumes the speed stays 6 m s⁻¹ throughout. Under constant deceleration the mean speed is 3 m s⁻¹, giving 6 m.

1. Distance equals speed times time only with a constant speed or the correct average speed.

2. The trolley slows from 6 to 0 during the interval.

3. For this linear speed change, the average is 3, so the distance is 6 m.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- The trolley slows from 6 to 0 during the interval.
