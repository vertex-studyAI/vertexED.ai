## Energy enters and is transferred

An ecosystem includes organisms and their physical environment, connected through exchanges of matter and energy. In many ecosystems, producers capture light energy through photosynthesis and store some of it in chemical forms. Consumers obtain chemical energy by feeding, while decomposers use organic material from dead organisms and wastes. A food-chain arrow conventionally points from the food resource toward the organism receiving material and energy from it. Real feeding relationships usually form a web, because organisms may consume several resources and occupy different feeding roles over their lives.

## Transfer is incomplete

Not all energy at one trophic level becomes biomass available to the next. Some material is not consumed, some consumed material is not assimilated, and assimilated energy supports respiration and other biological processes. Energy transferred to the surroundings as heat is not recycled into biological chemical energy by a closed trophic loop. Matter can be repeatedly exchanged through biogeochemical cycles, but ecosystems require continuing energy input. A fixed ten-percent rule is only a rough teaching approximation, not a universal efficiency for every organism, trophic link or ecosystem. Use the values and definitions supplied in the actual question.

## Productivity and units

Productivity is a rate of biomass or energy production, so its units include time and often an area or volume. Gross primary productivity describes the total rate of energy fixation by producers under the chosen definition. Net primary productivity subtracts the producers' respiratory use from gross production. Do not confuse a standing biomass stock at one moment with the rate at which new biomass is produced. Comparisons need compatible units, periods and system boundaries. A number reported per square metre per year cannot be compared directly with a whole-region total without accounting for area and time.

## Models, change and evidence

A food web helps predict possible pathways of influence, but a change in one population need not cause a single simple response everywhere else. Alternative foods, competition, seasonal changes and environmental conditions can modify the outcome. An energy pyramid concerns flows over a defined period; a biomass pyramid concerns a stock and can look different where producers turn over rapidly. Distinguish these quantities before interpreting a diagram. An ecological explanation should identify the proposed mechanism and the evidence needed to test it. Avoid treating a hypothetical numerical exercise as a measured account of an actual habitat, or a short-term observation as proof of a permanent ecosystem trend.

## Comparing productivity over a common area and time

Energy-transfer comparisons need matching units and boundaries. A transfer percentage compares energy incorporated at the next trophic level with the energy available at the previous one over the same area and interval. Gross primary productivity includes the energy fixed by producers before their respiratory use is subtracted; net primary productivity is the remainder available for growth and other transfers. Not all net production is consumed by herbivores, and not all consumed energy becomes herbivore biomass. Keep these stages distinct rather than using one unexplained ten-percent rule. The example values are fictional and do not represent a measured ecosystem or a universal ecological constant.

## Worked example 1

A producer level stores 2000 units of energy over a defined interval, and 150 reach the next level as new biomass. Calculate the stated transfer efficiency.

1. Use energy received divided by energy available under the question's definition.

2. Calculate 150/2000×100.

3. Keep both energy quantities within the same interval and boundary.

Answer: 7.5%.

## Worked example 2

Gross primary productivity is 900 energy units per area per year and producer respiration is 350 in matching units. Find net primary productivity.

1. Use NPP=GPP−producer respiration.

2. Subtract 350 from 900.

3. Retain the rate and area units.

Answer: 550 energy units per area per year.

## Worked example 3

Explain why a small producer biomass can support a larger consumer biomass at a particular moment in some aquatic systems.

1. A biomass measurement is a standing stock, not a production rate.

2. Rapid producer growth and consumption can renew a small stock repeatedly.

3. The observation does not reverse the overall direction of energy transfer.

Answer: Rapid turnover can produce an inverted standing-biomass pattern without an inverted energy-flow budget.

## Worked example 4

A fictional ecosystem has GPP of 18,000 kJ m⁻² year⁻¹ and producer respiration of 7,500 in the same units. Herbivore production is 840. Find NPP and herbivore production as a percentage of NPP.

1. Subtract producer respiration from GPP: 18,000 - 7,500 = 10,500.

2. Use NPP as the stated denominator: 840/10,500 × 100.

3. The result is 8%, with area and time units matched.

Answer: NPP is 10,500 kJ m⁻² year⁻¹; herbivore production is 8% of NPP.

## Misconceptions

- Energy cycles through an ecosystem in the same way as matter.
  Correction: Energy flows through transformations and is dissipated to the surroundings; continued input is needed.

- Every trophic transfer is exactly ten percent efficient.
  Correction: Efficiency varies; calculate from specified data rather than imposing a universal value.

## Practice 4 — extension · 3 marks

Explain why a decrease in one prey population does not guarantee that every predator in its food web declines immediately.

Answer: Predators may use alternative foods, differ in their dependence on that prey, or respond after a delay. A food-web link identifies a possible pathway, not a complete quantitative prediction under all conditions.

1. Identify variation in dependence or alternative resources.

2. Consider timing or other environmental influences.

3. Limit the prediction to what the model supports.

## Practice 7 — extension · 3 marks

A learner says the remaining 92% of NPP was all destroyed. Explain two reasons this interpretation is wrong.

Answer: Energy is transferred, not destroyed; some NPP is not eaten by herbivores, and some consumed energy is transferred through respiration or waste rather than new biomass.

1. Conservation of energy rules out destruction as the explanation.

2. Uneaten plant material can enter other pathways such as detrital food webs.

3. Consumed energy can be transferred as heat through respiration or leave in waste rather than herbivore production.
