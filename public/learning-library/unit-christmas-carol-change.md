## Isolation establishes a problem of relationship

Stave One presents Scrooge’s rejection of charitable appeals and his nephew’s invitation alongside his treatment of Bob Cratchit. These encounters make isolation social rather than merely a preference for quiet. Marley’s ghost warns through a material image of burdens accumulated in life: the supernatural creates a way to make unseen moral consequences visible. The narrator’s lively interventions also shape response, inviting judgement without requiring readers to adopt Scrooge’s perspective.

## The past makes alternative selves imaginable

The Ghost of Christmas Past returns Scrooge to childhood loneliness, Fezziwig’s celebration and the breakdown of his relationship with Belle. The sequence does more than provide biography. Loneliness can make his pain understandable; Fezziwig models an employer whose generosity produces shared enjoyment; Belle’s departure connects emotional loss with changing priorities. These episodes complicate a fixed label of natural cruelty, showing choices and experiences that helped produce the present man.

## The present challenges abstraction

The Cratchit family’s celebration gives particular faces and relationships to people whom Scrooge has treated in economic abstractions. Tiny Tim’s vulnerability makes indifference harder to maintain, but the family should not be reduced to props for Scrooge’s improvement: their affection also exposes values his wealth has not secured. Ignorance and Want widen the argument from one household to social neglect. A strong analysis explains how named or personified figures transform a general problem into an encounter readers must interpret.

## The future and the ending test change

The final spirit offers visions of an unlamented death, opportunistic responses to possessions and the Cratchits’ loss. The withheld identity of the dead man lets Scrooge approach recognition gradually; his own grave makes the warning personal. These visions present a possible future that gives present choice urgency, not an unavoidable fate that makes repentance pointless.

Stave Five verifies change through behaviour: generosity, renewed family connection and improved treatment of Bob. The movement from private fear to sustained social action makes the moral conclusion more substantial than a promise. Victorian debates about poverty and responsibility help frame the novella, but avoid asserting that one fictional transformation solves structural poverty. Interpret the ending as a narrative proposal for responsibility while recognising the limits of individual charity.

## Worked example 1

Explain why Fezziwig matters beyond being a cheerful employer.

1. Identify the workplace setting and celebration.

2. Connect the memory to Scrooge’s present power over Bob.

Answer: He provides a remembered model of authority whose modest actions create collective pleasure, challenging Scrooge’s narrow valuation of expenditure.

## Worked example 2

How can the final stave answer doubt about Scrooge’s promises?

1. Distinguish an emotional declaration from conduct.

2. Use the changed treatment of Bob or renewed family contact as evidence.

Answer: It shows repeated actions affecting others, converting stated intention into observable change.

## Misconceptions

- The spirits only frighten Scrooge.
  Correction: They also use memory, empathy, contrast and recognition.

- The future vision proves that events cannot change.
  Correction: Its warning creates the possibility and urgency of a different course.

## Flashcards

### What does Marley make visible?
The consequences of a life organised around selfish priorities.

### Why revisit Fezziwig?
To model generous authority and connect it to Scrooge’s employer role.

### What changes with the Cratchit visit?
Abstract poverty becomes particular human relationships.

### Why delay recognition of the dead man?
To structure discovery and intensify self-recognition.

### How is transformation tested?
By action towards other people, not feeling alone.

## Practice 5 — standard · 3 marks

Answer: A possible future makes present decisions consequential; an inevitable one would undermine the value of reform.

Marking points (one practice mark each):
