# Research questions, source selection and a defensible argument

Subject: Extended Essay · Unit: Research planning · Revision: 2026-10-21.4

**Review draft — not independently approved. Practice marks are editorial, not official exam marks.**

Original shared study module on research questions, source selection and a defensible argument. Numerical datasets and quoted passages are invented teaching examples. This is not a complete course, official examination material or verified board-specific alignment.

## Prerequisites

- Distinguish a topic from a focused question.

- Keep track of sources and evidence.

## Objectives

- Narrow a question to an answerable scope.

- Choose evidence suited to a method and claim.

- Separate a supported conclusion from a predetermined outcome.

## A question sets the work's boundaries

A topic names an area of interest; a research question identifies what is to be investigated within it. A feasible question specifies enough context, time, material or variables to guide evidence collection. Narrowing is not merely making a title longer. Ask what evidence would count for or against a proposed answer and whether that evidence can actually be accessed. A question that assumes its own conclusion can bias the entire investigation. State the intended comparison or relationship without presuming that a particular effect must exist.

## Choose evidence before promising a conclusion

Different questions call for different materials and methods. Comparing published interpretations requires careful selection and attribution; estimating an experimental effect requires suitable observations and controls. Access, time, equipment and ethical constraints affect feasibility. Do not collect identifiable information or involve people in a study without the required consent and institutional procedures. A source log should record enough information to find the material again, distinguish quotation from paraphrase, and note relevant limitations. A convenient source is not automatically representative or sufficient for the research question.

## Develop and revise the argument

Organise the argument around claims that answer the research question, with evidence and reasoning for each. Distinguish results from interpretation and include limitations that affect the conclusion's strength. Negative or mixed findings can be informative; do not alter the question after seeing results and present the altered version as the original plan. If the question changes, document the change and its reason. ## Worked example 1

Narrow the broad topic 'transport in cities' into a question answerable with an available monthly bus dataset for one town.

1. Specify place, period and measured quantity rather than all urban transport.

2. Use a question such as: How did recorded monthly bus journeys in Town Q change between January and December in the supplied year?

Answer: A bounded descriptive question tied to the available data; it does not yet establish a causal explanation.

## Worked example 2

A fictional dataset shows a decrease when a policy began. Distinguish a result from an interpretation.

1. The decrease and its timing are descriptive results.

2. Claiming the policy caused it requires evidence about alternative explanations and an appropriate comparison.

Answer: Report the change separately from a causal hypothesis.

## Misconceptions

- A strong investigation must confirm the initial expectation.
  Correction: The conclusion should follow the evidence, including negative or mixed results.

- A before-and-after change alone proves the intervention caused it.
  Correction: Other time-varying factors and comparison evidence matter.

## Flashcards

### How does a research question differ from a topic?
It specifies an answerable investigation within the broader area.

### What makes a question feasible?
Accessible evidence, suitable methods, manageable scope and required ethical permissions.

### Why keep a source log?
To preserve traceability, attribution and source limitations.

### Are negative findings automatically a failed investigation?
No. They can inform the question if the method and interpretation are sound.

### How should a changed research question be handled?
Record the change and rationale rather than hiding the revision.

## Practice 1 — standard · 2 marks

Rewrite the broad topic 'social media and language' as a bounded textual-analysis question using only a supplied collection of 40 public posts by one organisation in one month.

Answer: Example: How does the organisation use questions and direct address in its 40 supplied posts during that month?

1. Specify the corpus and time boundary.

2. Choose observable language features rather than claiming effects on all readers.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Bound the evidence and period.

- Ask about an analysable feature.

## Practice 2 — standard · 2 marks

What is problematic about 'Why did the new policy inevitably improve attendance?' before attendance data have been analysed?

Answer: It assumes both improvement and inevitability before examining evidence.

1. Identify the presupposed result.

2. Use a neutral question about whether and how recorded attendance changed, with causal claims treated separately.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Identify the built-in conclusion.

- Offer a neutral reformulation.

## Practice 3 — extension · 2 marks

A project asks about historical public opinion but has only one politician's speech. Explain the evidence gap.

Answer: The speech shows one public position, not the full distribution of public opinion.

1. Match the source's scope to the question.

2. Seek a justified range of contemporary evidence and evaluate whose views are missing.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- State the mismatch.

- Identify a broader evidence strategy.

## Practice 4 — extension · 2 marks

An investigation's first two measurements support the hypothesis and the next three do not. What should be reported?

Answer: All five relevant measurements, the method, uncertainty and a conclusion reflecting the mixed evidence.

1. Do not select only favourable observations.

2. Investigate any documented measurement problem without excluding results merely for being inconvenient.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Retain the complete relevant evidence.

- Give a proportionate conclusion and handling of errors.
