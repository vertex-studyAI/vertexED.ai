## Defeat and a contested beginning

By autumn 1918 military defeat, shortages and war weariness undermined the German monarchy. Naval mutiny and revolution accompanied the Kaiser’s abdication in November; a republic was declared and the armistice followed on 11 November. Civilian leaders inherited defeat rather than causing it simply by signing. The later claim that the army had been betrayed at home shifted blame away from military failure and damaged democratic legitimacy.

The 1919 constitution introduced a parliamentary republic, elected representation and a powerful elected president. Proportional representation included diverse political voices but could produce fragmented parliaments and unstable coalitions. Emergency presidential powers were a potential weakness, not an automatic explanation for every later event. Institutions operated within conflict over the new state’s legitimacy.

## Threats did not all come from the same side

In January 1919 the Spartacist uprising challenged the government from the revolutionary left; the government relied on Freikorps violence to suppress it. This helped immediate survival but deepened divisions on the left. In March 1920 the right-wing Kapp Putsch exposed doubts about military loyalty. A general strike helped defeat it. Comparing these episodes shows that the republic survived through different alliances and pressures rather than through unchallenged constitutional authority.

## The 1923 crisis and inflation

French and Belgian occupation of the Ruhr in January 1923 followed disputes over reparations deliveries. The German government encouraged passive resistance, while paying workers and losing production and revenue. Money creation intensified inflation that already had roots in wartime finance and post-war deficits. Hyperinflation eroded fixed incomes and savings, while some debtors could repay old debts in devalued currency. Therefore it is inaccurate to say that every German lost in precisely the same way.

Stresemann ended passive resistance in September 1923. The Rentenmark was introduced in November, alongside fiscal and political measures that helped stabilisation. The Munich Putsch in November failed, but its failure alone did not remove extremist hostility. The Dawes Plan belongs to 1924: it must not be used as an event explaining how a November 1923 action occurred.

## Causation and interpretation

To explain survival, weigh organised resistance to putsches, government decisions, opponents’ weaknesses and improving conditions. A statement that economic crisis mattered is not yet a causal explanation: identify who was affected, how their behaviour changed and what political consequence followed. When comparing interpretations, first identify their different claims, then test each with relevant knowledge. Provenance can explain perspective, but an author’s date or identity cannot replace evaluating the argument itself.

## Worked example 1

Explain one political consequence of the government’s response to the Spartacist uprising.

1. Identify the reliance on Freikorps suppression in January 1919.

2. Connect the violence to divisions rather than merely stating that fighting occurred.

Answer: It preserved the government in the short term while deepening hostility between parts of the left.

## Worked example 2

Assess the claim: Ruhr occupation alone caused hyperinflation.

1. Link occupation and passive resistance to lost output, state payments and money creation.

2. Recognise earlier wartime and post-war financial pressures, so the absolute word alone is too strong.

Answer: It intensified the crisis but was not its only cause.

## Misconceptions

- The republic created military defeat by signing the armistice.
  Correction: The new civilian government inherited a failing military situation.

- The Dawes Plan ended the Ruhr crisis in 1923.
  Correction: The Dawes Plan was agreed in 1924; ending passive resistance and currency stabilisation began in 1923.
