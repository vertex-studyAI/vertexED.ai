## Demand is a relationship

Demand describes quantities buyers are willing and able to purchase at different prices during a specified period, other relevant conditions held constant. Quantity demanded is one value on that relationship. A change in the good's own price produces a movement along an unchanged demand curve, whereas changes in income, preferences or related-good prices can shift the curve. The direction of an income effect depends on whether the good is normal or inferior in the context. Avoid saying that every increase in income necessarily raises demand for every good. Label price and quantity axes, units and the market being modelled.

## Supply and market clearing

Supply describes quantities sellers are willing and able to offer at different prices under stated conditions. Costs, technology and the number of sellers can shift supply. The equilibrium of a simple competitive model occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At a price above equilibrium, the model predicts excess supply; below equilibrium, excess demand. These pressures describe a mechanism under the model's assumptions, not a guarantee that every real price instantly adjusts. Contracts, regulation, market power, information and time lags may alter the observed process. A demand or supply curve is not a time-series graph of price changes.

## Comparing before and after

Comparative statics compares equilibria before and after a specified change while holding other influences fixed. An increase in demand with unchanged upward-sloping supply raises equilibrium price and quantity in the usual model. An increase in supply with unchanged downward-sloping demand lowers price and raises quantity. If both curves shift, some outcomes can be ambiguous without information about relative shift sizes. For example, increases in both demand and supply raise equilibrium quantity in the standard model but do not determine the direction of price. A complete explanation names the initial condition, identifies the changed determinant, shifts the appropriate curve and follows the adjustment mechanism to a qualified conclusion.

## Simultaneous changes and justified predictions

A demand or supply diagram is a model with stated conditions, not a record of every cause operating in a market. When one curve shifts and the other remains fixed, the direction of the new equilibrium changes can often be predicted. When both shift, one outcome may be clear while another depends on the relative sizes of the shifts. Do not choose a definite price direction merely because one familiar diagram showed it.

If demand increases and supply also increases, equilibrium quantity rises under the usual upward-supply and downward-demand assumptions, but the price change is ambiguous without more information. Distinguish an observed change in price from a shift in demand: observing higher price and quantity is compatible with increased demand, but does not prove that no supply change occurred. A strong explanation states the cause, identifies the affected curve, describes the initial excess demand or supply at the old price, and then traces adjustment toward a new equilibrium. Elasticity, timing and market restrictions can affect the response; a basic diagram should not silently claim to capture them all.

## Distinguishing a curve shift from a response along a curve

A change in the good’s own price changes quantity demanded along a fixed demand relationship. A change in another determinant, such as income or preferences, can shift that relationship. In a comparative-static model, write the original and revised demand equations explicitly while holding the supply equation fixed if that is the assumption. Solve each equilibrium separately, then compare both price and quantity. The movement along the unchanged supply curve is a response to the new equilibrium price, not evidence that supply shifted. These equations are fictional teaching models. They do not imply that an actual market adjusts instantly or that every relevant determinant has been included.

## Worked example 1

In a hypothetical market Qd=100-2P and Qs=20+2P. Find equilibrium.

1. Set quantities equal: 100-2P=20+2P.

2. Solve 80=4P to obtain P=20.

3. Substitute into either equation to obtain Q=60.

Answer: Equilibrium price 20 and quantity 60 in the stated units.

## Worked example 2

Input costs fall while demand stays unchanged. Explain the usual equilibrium effects.

1. Lower costs allow more output to be supplied at a given price.

2. Supply shifts right, creating excess supply at the old price.

3. Under the standard adjustment model, price falls and equilibrium quantity rises.

Answer: Lower price and higher quantity, conditional on the model and unchanged demand.

## Worked example 3

Demand increases while supply is unchanged. Explain the adjustment from the old equilibrium.

1. At the old price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.

2. The shortage creates upward pressure on price in the simple competitive model.

3. As price rises, quantity supplied increases and quantity demanded moves along the new demand curve toward equality.

Answer: The new equilibrium has a higher price and quantity under the stated model.

## Worked example 4

In a fictional market, Qd = 100 - 2P and Qs = 10 + P. Demand then becomes Qd = 130 - 2P while supply stays fixed. Find both equilibria.

1. Equate original demand and supply: 100 - 2P = 10 + P, giving P = 30.

2. Substitution gives Q = 40.

3. For revised demand, 130 - 2P = 10 + P gives P = 40 and Q = 50.

Answer: Initially P = 30 and Q = 40; afterwards P = 40 and Q = 50.

## Misconceptions

- A price fall shifts demand right.
  Correction: A change in the good's own price moves along an unchanged demand curve.

- Two simultaneous curve shifts always determine the direction of price.
  Correction: Relative sizes can matter; some outcomes remain ambiguous.
