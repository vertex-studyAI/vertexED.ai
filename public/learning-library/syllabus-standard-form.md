## Both tiers: normalisation and conversion

For a positive quantity, standard form is A×10^n with 1≤A<10 and integer n. Move the decimal point until one nonzero digit lies before it, then choose the power that restores the original size. Thus 62,000=6.2×10⁴ while 0.00062=6.2×10⁻⁴. A positive exponent makes the value large, and a negative exponent makes it small. The exponent is a scale factor, not a count of all zeros in the printed number.

9.8×10³ is normalised; 98×10² is equal to it but is not in standard form. Ten itself becomes 1×10¹, not 10×10⁰. For negative quantities keep the minus sign outside the normalised positive magnitude, for example −(3.2×10⁵). To compare positive standard-form values, first compare exponents, then coefficients if the exponents match. Normalisation is what makes this ordering shortcut valid.

## Both tiers: multiplication, division and addition

Multiply coefficients and add exponents; divide coefficients and subtract exponents. Then normalise again: (6×10⁵)(4×10⁻²)=24×10³=2.4×10⁴. An intermediate coefficient below 1 must also be repaired: (2×10³)/(8×10⁶)=0.25×10⁻³=2.5×10⁻⁴. Each decimal shift in the coefficient is balanced by an opposite change in the exponent.

For addition or subtraction, make the powers of ten equal first. 3.1×10⁶+4×10⁵=(3.1+0.4)×10⁶=3.5×10⁶. Adding the exponents here would be wrong: index multiplication laws do not apply to addition. Cambridge Core learners practise standard-form calculations on the calculator Paper 3; Extended and GCSE learners also need methods appropriate to their paper and question.

## Both tiers: calculator display, dimensions and checks

A display such as 7.2E−5 usually means 7.2×10⁻⁵, not 7.2 minus 5. Use the calculator scientific-notation key as its instructions require; do not type an extra multiplication by ten if the entry already supplies it. Parenthesise an entire denominator when entering a ratio. Preserve unrounded intermediate values, then round the final coefficient to the requested significant figures.

Units still matter. If a sheet has thickness 2.5×10⁻⁴ m and there are 8×10³ sheets, multiplying gives 2 m, not a dimensionless answer. Estimate the magnitude independently: about ten thousand sheets each a fraction of a millimetre plausibly make metres. Dividing a length by a time produces a speed; the exponent cannot compensate for incompatible units.

## Worked example 1

Convert 0.000078 and 405,000,000 to standard form.

1. Shift the first decimal five places right to obtain 7.8, so use 10⁻⁵ to restore the small value.

2. Shift the second decimal eight places left to obtain 4.05, so use 10⁸.

Answer: 7.8×10⁻⁵ and 4.05×10⁸.

## Worked example 2

Calculate (3.6×10⁷)/(9×10⁻³) and 4.2×10⁵−8×10⁴.

1. The quotient is 0.4×10¹⁰; normalise to 4×10⁹.

2. Write the subtraction as (4.2−0.8)×10⁵.

Answer: 4×10⁹ and 3.4×10⁵.

## Misconceptions

- Add exponents when adding numbers in standard form.
  Correction: Only multiplication of common-base powers adds exponents; addition requires a common scale.

- A coefficient of 10 is allowed in standard form.
  Correction: The upper limit is strict: change 10×10^n to 1×10^(n+1).

## Flashcards

1≤A<10 for a positive magnitude.

### What does E−6 mean on a scientific display?
Multiply by 10⁻⁶.

### How are powers handled in division?
Subtract denominator exponent from numerator exponent.

### What must match before addition?
The powers of ten.

### How do you normalise 0.7×10⁴?
7×10³.

## Practice 3 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Order 9.9×10⁻⁴, 1.1×10⁻³ and 8×10⁻⁵ from smallest to largest.

Answer: 8×10⁻⁵ < 9.9×10⁻⁴ < 1.1×10⁻³.

1. All coefficients are normalised and positive.

2. Compare exponents −5, −4 and −3, or convert to decimals.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Correctly identify the smallest.

- Place the remaining two in order.

## Practice 4 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Calculate (7.5×10⁻³)(4×10⁶) in standard form. IGCSE Core: Paper 3 practice.

Answer: 3×10⁴.

1. 7.5×4=30 and −3+6=3.

2. Normalise 30×10³ to 3×10⁴.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Obtain 30×10³.

- Normalise correctly.

## Practice 5 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Calculate (1.2×10⁻²)/(6×10⁴) in standard form. IGCSE Core: Paper 3 practice.

Answer: 2×10⁻⁷.

1. Divide coefficients to get 0.2 and subtract exponents to get −6.

2. Rewrite 0.2×10⁻⁶ as 2×10⁻⁷.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Use exponent −6 before normalising.

- Obtain 2×10⁻⁷.

## Practice 6 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] Find 5.3×10⁶+7×10⁵ in standard form. Explain why 12.3×10¹¹ is not a valid result. IGCSE Core: Paper 3 practice.

Answer: 6×10⁶; exponents are not added in a sum.

1. Rewrite 7×10⁵ as 0.7×10⁶.

2. Add coefficients on the shared scale: 5.3+0.7=6.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Use a common power of ten.

- Obtain 6×10⁶ and reject the multiplication rule.

## Practice 7 — standard · 2 marks

[GCSE Foundation/Higher; IGCSE Core/Extended] A calculator displays 2.4E−4 for a wire diameter in metres. Write the diameter in millimetres and explain the display.

Answer: 0.24 mm; E−4 means ×10⁻⁴.

1. The diameter is 0.00024 m.

2. Multiply metres by 1000 to get millimetres.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- Interpret the scientific display.

- Convert to 0.24 mm.
