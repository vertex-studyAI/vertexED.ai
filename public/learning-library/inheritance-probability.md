# Alleles, segregation and probability in monohybrid models

## What an allele represents

A gene is a DNA sequence associated with a functional product, while alleles are alternative versions at a locus. In the simplified diploid model used here, an individual carries two alleles at the locus, one inherited from each parent. A homozygote has two matching alleles and a heterozygote has two different alleles. Genotype refers to the allele combination; phenotype is an observable characteristic influenced by genotype and often the environment. Complete dominance means the heterozygote has the same phenotype category as one homozygote for the trait being considered. Dominant does not mean more common, more beneficial or stronger in a general biological sense.

## Segregation and combining gametes

During gamete formation the two alleles separate, so each gamete receives one allele in this model. A heterozygote Aa produces A and a gametes in equal expected proportions, whereas AA produces only A gametes. A Punnett square combines possible gametes from the two parents. Its cells represent equally likely fertilisation combinations only when the row and column probabilities justify that assumption. For Aa crossed with Aa, the expected genotype probabilities are one quarter AA, one half Aa and one quarter aa. With complete dominance of A, those correspond to three quarters dominant phenotype and one quarter recessive phenotype.

## Interpreting predictions responsibly

An expected ratio summarises probabilities over many comparable events. Four offspring from an Aa by Aa cross are not guaranteed to include exactly one aa individual. Under independent fertilisation, the probability of two successive aa offspring is one quarter multiplied by one quarter, or one sixteenth. A previous offspring's genotype does not change the next event's probability in the stated model. To infer an unknown dominant-phenotype genotype, a test cross with aa can be informative: recessive offspring exclude AA under the simple assumptions. Small samples containing only dominant offspring cannot prove AA with certainty. Real inheritance may involve multiple genes, sex linkage, incomplete dominance, penetrance and environmental effects, so do not apply this model universally.

## Model assumptions, sample size and inference

A Punnett square describes possible allele combinations under a specified inheritance model; its boxes are not a guarantee that the next small family will contain each category once. Each independent offspring is another probability trial. The expected count in a sample is sample size multiplied by the relevant probability, but an observed count can differ through random variation. A larger sample often gives a more stable estimate of a proportion, although it does not repair a wrong biological model.

Simple single-gene models assume a defined relationship between genotype and phenotype, ordinary segregation and the stated parental genotypes. Real traits may involve multiple genes, environmental effects, linkage or other complications. Dominance describes phenotype expression in a heterozygote; it does not mean an allele is more common, stronger, beneficial or guaranteed to increase over generations. When using offspring data to infer a parent's genotype, compare the possible explanations and their probabilities. One observation incompatible with a model can be highly informative, while several observations compatible with it may still fail to prove it uniquely. State the model rather than presenting a simplified cross as a universal account of inheritance.

## Using a test cross without treating a small sample as certainty

Under a simple complete-dominance model, a dominant phenotype may correspond to either a homozygous or a heterozygous genotype. Crossing that individual with a homozygous recessive partner can distinguish the predictions. A heterozygous parent produces recessive-phenotype offspring with probability one half in this model; a homozygous dominant parent does not. However, observing only dominant offspring in a small sample is compatible with either parental genotype. Calculate the chance of the observation under each hypothesis and keep probability distinct from proof. The example assumes independent offspring, ordinary segregation and no viability differences; it is not a description of every inherited trait.

## Condition on the information actually observed

A probability changes when the question restricts which offspring are being considered. In an Aa×Aa cross with complete dominance, the whole sample has genotype ratio 1:2:1. Among only the dominant-phenotype offspring, aa is excluded, so the denominator contains AA and Aa alone. Independence across offspring means a previous result does not change the model for the next offspring when parental genotypes are fixed. Expected proportions describe a probability model, not a requirement that each small family reproduces the exact ratio. These are simplified single-gene teaching models.

## Worked example 1

Cross Aa with aa under complete dominance of A. Give genotype and phenotype probabilities.

1. Aa produces A or a gametes, each with probability 1/2.

2. aa produces only a gametes.

3. Combining gives Aa with probability 1/2 and aa with probability 1/2.

Answer: Half expected Aa with dominant phenotype and half aa with recessive phenotype.

## Worked example 2

For independent offspring of Aa×Aa, find the probability that neither of two offspring is aa.

1. For one offspring, probability of not aa is 3/4.

2. Multiply for two independent events: (3/4)².

3. Do not subtract 1/4 twice; those events can overlap.

Answer: 9/16.

## Worked example 3

For an Aa×aa cross under complete dominance, find the expected number of recessive-phenotype offspring in 20 independent trials.

1. The recessive genotype aa has probability 1/2 per offspring.

2. Expected count is 20×1/2=10.

3. An actual sample need not contain exactly 10 recessive offspring.

Answer: Expected count 10, not a guaranteed outcome.

## Worked example 4

In a complete-dominance model, an Aa individual is crossed with aa. Give the offspring genotype probabilities and the probability that all three independent offspring show the dominant phenotype.

1. The Aa parent supplies A or a with equal probability; the aa parent supplies only a.

2. The two equally likely offspring genotypes are Aa and aa.

3. Three independent dominant outcomes have probability (1/2)³ = 1/8.

Answer: P(Aa) = 1/2 and P(aa) = 1/2; the probability all three show the dominant phenotype is 1/8.

## Misconceptions

- Dominant alleles must be common in the population.
  Correction: Dominance describes heterozygote phenotype, not allele frequency.

- A 3:1 expected ratio guarantees three dominant offspring in every four.
  Correction: Actual small samples fluctuate; the ratio is probabilistic.

## Flashcards

### How do genotype and phenotype differ?
Genotype is the allele combination; phenotype is the observable characteristic influenced by genotype and other factors.

### What does heterozygous mean?
Two different alleles at the locus in the diploid model.

### What allele does a gamete receive from an Aa parent?
A or a, each with expected probability one half in this model.

### What does complete dominance describe?
The heterozygote shares the phenotype category of one homozygote.

### Does one aa offspring make another aa offspring less likely in an independent Aa×Aa cross?
No. Each offspring still has probability one quarter of being aa.

### What does an expected count describe?
The probability-weighted average over repeated samples of the specified size, not a guaranteed count in one sample.

### What makes aa useful in a simple test cross?
It supplies only recessive alleles, exposing the other parent’s possible gamete contributions.

### Do three dominant offspring prove a parent is homozygous dominant?
No. A heterozygous parent can produce that finite outcome by chance.

## Practice 4 — extension · 3 marks

An unknown dominant-phenotype parent crossed with aa produces three dominant-phenotype offspring. Does this prove the unknown parent is AA? Explain using probabilities.

Answer: No. If the parent were Aa, three dominant-phenotype offspring would still occur with probability (1/2)³=1/8 under independence. The observations are compatible with AA but do not establish it uniquely.

1. Identify AA and Aa as possible dominant-phenotype genotypes.

2. Calculate the probability of the observed outcome under Aa×aa.

3. Distinguish evidence favouring a possibility from proof.

- Conclude that three dominant offspring do not prove AA.

## Practice 7 — extension · 3 marks

An unknown dominant-phenotype parent crossed with aa has three dominant-phenotype offspring. A learner says this proves the parent is AA. Explain the error using the alternative genotype.

Answer: Aa remains possible: it produces three dominant offspring with probability 1/8 under the stated model.

1. The dominant phenotype permits AA or Aa before the cross.

2. Three dominant outcomes are certain under AA but still possible under Aa.

3. An outcome compatible with both hypotheses does not prove one genotype.

## Practice 8 — extension · 3 marks

In Aa×Aa with complete dominance and equal segregation, an offspring is known to show the dominant phenotype. Find the probability it is heterozygous.

Answer: 2/3.

1. The possible genotypes have probabilities AA=1/4, Aa=1/2, aa=1/4.

2. Condition on AA or Aa: (1/2)/(3/4)=2/3.

Marking points (one practice mark each):

- State the original genotype probabilities.

- Exclude aa and use denominator 3/4.

- Find 2/3.

## Practice 9 — standard · 3 marks

2. There are three possible positions for the aa offspring.

## Practice 10 — standard · 3 marks

A fixed Aa×aa model gives P(aa)=1/2. The first four offspring are all Aa. What is P(aa) for the fifth, and why is aa not now guaranteed?

Answer: It remains 1/2 under independence. The model does not force outcomes to compensate for an earlier streak.

1. The parental genotypes and segregation probabilities have not changed.

2. Independence means the fifth event is not determined by the first four.
